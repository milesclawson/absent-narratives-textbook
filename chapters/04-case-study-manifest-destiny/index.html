    Case Study: Manifest Destiny¶

    Summary¶

    This case-study chapter applies Chapters 1-3's tools to a single, concrete example: Manifest Destiny and westward expansion. Students trace how the frontier myth and American exceptionalism functioned as a dominant narrative that obscured Indigenous displacement, the Trail of Tears, and land dispossession under the Homestead Act. The chapter closes by asking students to identify the erased Indigenous voices in the standard textbook account — a model they will reuse throughout the rest of the course.

    Why This Chapter Is a Case Study, Not a New Unit¶

    Every chapter from here forward applies the tools built in Chapters 1-3 to a specific slice of history. This chapter goes first because its subject — Manifest Destiny — is a dominant narrative most American students have already partially absorbed by tenth grade, which makes it an ideal place to practice recognizing a dominant narrative from the inside, before Chapter 5 asks you to spot less familiar ones in government policy.

    Manifest Destiny was the 19th-century belief, widely stated in American newspapers, sermons, and political speeches, that the United States was destined — by divine right or by the natural order of history — to expand its territory across the North American continent. Notice the structure of that belief: it does not describe a fact about the land. It describes a claim about entitlement, dressed in the language of inevitability.

    That claim justified westward expansion: the 19th-century movement of U.S. settlers into land west of the original colonies, driven by government policy, economic incentive, and the belief system of Manifest Destiny. Textbooks often narrate westward expansion primarily through the experience of the settlers who moved — wagon trains, homesteads, "opening" the frontier. This chapter asks a different question of the same events: opening the frontier for whom, and at whose expense?

    The Frontier Myth and American Exceptionalism¶

    The settler-centered version of westward expansion did not emerge by accident. It reflects the frontier myth: the cultural narrative portraying westward expansion as a story of rugged individualism, opportunity, and civilization advancing into "empty" or "wild" land. The word "empty" belongs in quotation marks deliberately — the land crossed by westward expansion was inhabited by hundreds of distinct Indigenous nations, each with its own governance, economy, and territory. Calling it empty was not a factual description; it was a rhetorical move that made displacement look like discovery.

    The frontier myth drew its emotional force from a broader belief: American exceptionalism, the idea that the United States possesses a unique and superior national character or mission that sets it apart from, and often above, other nations. Applied to westward expansion, American exceptionalism reframed the acquisition of Indigenous land not as conquest — a word applied to other nations' territorial expansion — but as a natural, almost inevitable unfolding of American destiny. This is dominant narrative at work exactly as Chapter 1 defined it: not false in every particular, but constructed to make one group's gain look like an act of nature rather than a series of specific, contestable choices.

    Before we trace what the frontier myth left out, it helps to name the pattern precisely: this chapter's central case is an example of settler colonialism, a form of colonialism in which settlers permanently relocate to a territory and aim to replace the Indigenous population and its societies with a new society built by and for the settlers, rather than simply extracting resources and leaving. Settler colonialism is distinct from other forms of colonial control precisely because its goal is replacement, not administration — which is why westward expansion did not stop at treaties, but continued until Indigenous nations had lost the overwhelming majority of their original territory.

    What the Frontier Myth Obscured¶

    Indigenous displacement — the forced removal of Indigenous peoples from their ancestral homelands, carried out through treaties made under duress, military force, or policies designed to make continued residence impossible — was not a side effect of westward expansion. It was expansion's mechanism. Land did not become available for settlers because it was empty; it became available because it was made empty, through specific, documented policy decisions.

    The most infamous single episode of Indigenous displacement is the Trail of Tears: the forced relocation, beginning in 1838, of the Cherokee Nation and other Southeastern tribes from their homelands to designated Indian Territory west of the Mississippi River, during which thousands died from exposure, disease, and starvation along the route. The Trail of Tears was not a natural disaster. It was the direct, foreseeable result of the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and its enforcement — a policy decision, not a tragedy that simply happened.

    Once Indigenous nations had been displaced, the resulting land needed a mechanism to transfer it into settler ownership. The Homestead Act of 1862 provided that mechanism: federal legislation granting 160 acres of public land to settlers who agreed to live on and cultivate it for five years, effectively distributing land recently taken from Indigenous nations to settler families at minimal cost. The word "public" in "public land" is doing significant rhetorical work in that sentence — the land was only "public" in the sense that the federal government had already claimed it, typically through treaties negotiated under military and economic pressure.

    Before you see the timeline that connects these events, it is worth naming the broader process they add up to: land dispossession, the systemic, policy-driven transfer of land ownership away from Indigenous peoples and toward settlers, carried out through a combination of treaty, law, and force rather than a single event. Land dispossession is the connecting thread between the Trail of Tears and the Homestead Act — one displaces, the other redistributes what displacement freed up.

    Recovering the Erased Indigenous Voices¶

    A textbook account built entirely from settler diaries, government land records, and railroad company reports will, no matter how carefully researched, reproduce erased Indigenous voices: the absence, in standard historical accounts of westward expansion, of Indigenous perspectives, testimony, and interpretations of the same events — not because Indigenous peoples did not record or narrate their own experiences, but because those records were less likely to be collected, translated, published, or cited by the historians who built the dominant narrative.

    This is historical silence (Chapter 1) and marginalization (Chapter 1) operating on a specific, well-documented historical episode. Recovering the erased voices is not a matter of imagination — Indigenous nations kept their own oral histories, negotiated and signed their own treaty records, and in many cases have documented, published accounts, from tribal historians and from descendants, of the same events the frontier myth narrates from the opposite direction. The erasure lies in which of those two record-sets a standard textbook chooses to cite.

    Practicing the recovery of an erased narrative is this chapter's central skill, and it previews the research methods you will formalize in Chapter 14's capstone project.

    A textbook says settlers 'peacefully purchased' land from a tribe in an 1850s treaty. What questions should you ask before accepting that description? Click to expand

    Ask who was present at the negotiation and whether the tribal representatives had genuine alternatives to signing — treaties negotiated under military presence or economic desperation are not comparable to a peaceful market transaction, even if the document uses the word "purchase." Ask what the treaty's actual terms were, and whether they were honored afterward — many 19th-century treaties were violated by the U.S. government within years of signing. Ask whether tribal oral histories or tribal government records of the same negotiation survive, and whether the textbook consulted them. Ask what happened to the tribe in the years immediately following the "purchase" — displacement that continued after a treaty was signed is strong evidence that the treaty did not reflect a genuinely voluntary, informed exchange.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Manifest Destiny and westward expansion were driven by a frontier myth reinforced by American exceptionalism — together, a dominant narrative that reframed conquest as destiny.
    • This chapter's case is a specific instance of settler colonialism: expansion aimed at replacement, not just resource extraction.
    • Indigenous displacement, exemplified by the Trail of Tears, and land redistribution through the Homestead Act were two connected stages of a single land dispossession mechanism — not isolated or coincidental events.
    • Recovering erased Indigenous voices requires actively seeking out tribal records and oral histories that standard accounts often did not consult — a skill this course will ask you to practice again and again.

    Chapter 5 turns from a single historical case study to a broader, ongoing pattern: how systemic bias shapes government representation itself.
