    Quiz: Representation in the Media¶

    Test your understanding of media representation, bias, and media literacy with these review questions.

    1. What is media representation, as defined in this chapter?¶

    1. A legal requirement that all media outlets employ a diverse staff
    2. The way films, television, news, advertising, and digital platforms portray different groups, in terms of how frequently they appear, in what roles, and with what accuracy relative to their actual presence and diversity in society
    3. A synonym for propaganda used specifically in entertainment contexts
    4. The total number of media companies operating in a given country
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. Media representation concerns how frequently groups appear across media, in what roles, and with what accuracy relative to their real presence and diversity, applying Chapter 2's abstract definition of representation to media specifically. Option A invents a legal mandate not discussed. Option C conflates representation with a distinct, deliberately deceptive concept. Option D describes an unrelated business statistic.

    Concept Tested: Media Representation

    2. How does the chapter distinguish whitewashing from tokenized casting?¶

    1. The two terms are interchangeable and describe the exact same casting practice
    2. Tokenized casting only occurs in news media, while whitewashing only occurs in film
    3. Whitewashing involves casting decisions and tokenized casting involves headline writing
    4. Tokenized casting includes a marginalized presence in a minimal way, while whitewashing actively replaces a marginalized presence that a story's own source material specified
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Tokenized casting includes a single marginalized character in an otherwise homogeneous cast, often narrowly defined, while whitewashing goes further by casting white actors in roles written for or based on people of color, replacing rather than minimally including. Option A denies the explicit distinction the chapter draws. Option B invents a media-type restriction not in the text. Option C mischaracterizes both practices as belonging to news rather than casting.

    Concept Tested: Whitewashing

    3. What is the key difference between colorism in media and beauty standard bias, according to this chapter?¶

    1. Colorism in media operates specifically along the lightness/darkness axis within groups, while beauty standard bias is the broader favoring of one narrow standard encompassing body type, facial features, and hair texture along with skin tone
    2. Colorism in media only occurs in advertising, while beauty standard bias only occurs in news
    3. The two terms describe entirely unrelated phenomena with no shared history
    4. Beauty standard bias is a legal category, while colorism in media is not
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. Colorism in media specifically concerns favoring lighter skin tone within a demographic group, while beauty standard bias is the broader phenomenon of a single narrow standard — including body type, facial features, and hair texture — being treated as universally attractive. Option B invents media-type restrictions not stated. Option C ignores the chapter's framing of both as related, building on Chapter 3. Option D invents a legal distinction the chapter does not make.

    Concept Tested: Beauty Standard Bias

    4. How does the chapter relate headline bias to the broader framing effect?¶

    1. Headline bias and the framing effect are unrelated, describing entirely different media phenomena
    2. The framing effect only applies to entertainment media, while headline bias only applies to advertising
    3. Headline bias is a visible symptom of the broader framing effect, which shapes audience interpretation through selection of details, word choices, and visual context, independent of whether any individual fact is false
    4. Headline bias requires factually false reporting, while the framing effect does not
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Headline bias is described as one visible symptom of the broader framing effect, and the chapter stresses that framing does not require lying — two entirely accurate stories can produce opposite audience impressions through which true facts are emphasized, omitted, or paired with which images. Option A denies the explicit relationship the chapter draws. Option B invents media-type restrictions not stated. Option D reverses the chapter's point that framing operates without requiring any false fact.

    Concept Tested: Framing Effect

    5. Why can algorithmic bias occur "even when no individual programmer intended discrimination," according to this chapter?¶

    1. Because algorithmic bias is legally required to be intentional in order to exist
    2. Because algorithms are incapable of producing unfair outcomes under any circumstances
    3. Because systematic and repeatable errors can arise from biased training data, biased design choices, or biased proxy measures, independent of any individual's conscious intent
    4. Because algorithmic bias only occurs when a single human editor makes an explicit decision
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. Algorithmic bias is defined as systematic and repeatable errors in a system's output that create unfair outcomes for particular groups, arising from biased training data, design choices, or proxy measures, even without any individual programmer's conscious intent to discriminate. Option A wrongly requires intent, contradicting the chapter's definition. Option B denies the entire premise of the concept. Option D contradicts the chapter's point that algorithmic bias often lacks a single identifiable human decision-maker.

    Concept Tested: Algorithmic Bias

    6. Two news outlets cover the same property-crime event; one headline uses the word "looting" and the other uses "finding supplies," based only on which group is pictured, while the underlying facts are identical. Which concept does this scenario apply?¶

    1. Algorithmic bias, because a computer system selected the headline text
    2. Source validity, because the outlets have different reputations
    3. Counter-storytelling, because a marginalized perspective is being centered
    4. Headline bias, the tendency of news headlines to use different language depending on the race, nationality, or social group involved in the story, even when the underlying facts are identical
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. This is the chapter's own documented example of headline bias: identical underlying facts described with sympathetic versus criminalizing word choice depending on the group pictured, with no difference in the actual reported events. Option A misapplies a concept about algorithmic systems, not human headline writing. Option B misapplies a concept about source trustworthiness rather than word choice. Option C misapplies a concept about constructive response rather than the bias itself.

    Concept Tested: Headline Bias

    7. A film adaptation casts a white actor in a leading role that was written for, and historically based on, a person of color, without changing any other story detail. Which concept does this scenario apply?¶

    1. Whitewashing, the practice of casting white actors in roles originally written for, or historically based on, people of color, or of altering a story's racial or ethnic context to center white characters
    2. Tokenized casting, because only one character's casting is in question
    3. Digital media bias, because the casting decision affects a streaming platform
    4. Propaganda, because the casting is designed to promote a political agenda
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is A. This scenario directly matches the chapter's definition of whitewashing: casting a white actor in a role written for or based on a person of color, replacing a marginalized presence the source material specified. Option B misapplies tokenized casting, which involves minimal inclusion rather than replacement. Option C misapplies a concept about platform-level, often algorithmic, bias. Option D misapplies a concept requiring deliberate ideological intent not described here.

    Concept Tested: Whitewashing

    8. A reader wants to determine whether a news story reflects ordinary framing bias or deliberate propaganda. Applying the chapter's guidance, what should the reader check first?¶

    1. Whether the story was published on a weekday or weekend
    2. The source's track record, methodology, transparency about sourcing, and independence from conflicts of interest — its source validity — before determining whether distortion appears selective, exaggerated, or deliberately false
    3. Whether the story includes any images at all
    4. Whether the story was written by a human or an algorithm
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is B. The chapter directs readers to check source validity — track record, methodology, transparency, and independence from conflicts of interest — as the tool for distinguishing ordinary framing bias, which every outlet exhibits to some degree, from propaganda's deliberate distortion. Option A and C raise details the chapter identifies as irrelevant to this distinction. Option D misapplies a check relevant to algorithmic bias, a separate concept.

    Concept Tested: Source Validity

    9. Why does the chapter argue that algorithmic bias "connects directly back to this chapter's earlier concepts"?¶

    1. Because algorithmic bias has no relationship to any concept discussed earlier in the chapter
    2. Because algorithms are specifically designed to eliminate stereotype in media entirely
    3. Because an algorithm trained on decades of stereotype-laden and whitewashed media content will tend to reproduce those same patterns at scale, faster and with less visible human accountability than a single editor's decision
    4. Because algorithmic bias only affects news headlines, not entertainment media
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is C. The chapter explains that an algorithm trained on media data already containing stereotype and whitewashing patterns will tend to reproduce those same patterns at scale, with less visible human accountability than an individual editor's single decision. Option A denies the explicit connection drawn. Option B contradicts the mechanism described, which reproduces rather than eliminates bias. Option D wrongly narrows algorithmic bias to one media type.

    Concept Tested: Algorithmic Bias

    10. Why does the chapter describe counter-storytelling as "the constructive half of media literacy" rather than simply a way of pointing out bias?¶

    1. Because counter-storytelling requires no engagement with marginalized perspectives at all
    2. Because counter-storytelling is identical to source validity checking
    3. Because counter-storytelling only applies to algorithmic bias, not human-produced media
    4. Because counter-storytelling is the deliberate practice of telling stories from marginalized perspectives to actively challenge dominant, stereotyped, or incomplete narratives, rather than only identifying or criticizing existing bias
    Show Answer

    The correct answer is D. Counter-storytelling is defined as the deliberate practice of telling stories from marginalized perspectives to challenge dominant or incomplete narratives, making it a constructive, generative response rather than only a diagnostic or critical one — and it previews the capstone research project in Chapter 14. Option A contradicts the definition's emphasis on centering marginalized perspectives. Option B conflates two distinct tools the chapter separates. Option C wrongly restricts the concept to one bias type.

    Concept Tested: Counter-Storytelling
