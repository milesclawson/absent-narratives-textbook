    Language and Power¶

    Summary¶

    This short chapter examines the connection between language and power: linguistic suppression, code-switching, language revitalization, dialect bias, and the relationship between naming and identity. Students study oral tradition as a historical source in its own right and consider translation bias as one more lens on the author-perspective problem introduced in Chapter 1, setting up the oral-history research methods used in the capstone chapter that follows.

    The Chapter's Central Claim¶

    This short chapter makes one central claim and spends its length demonstrating it: language and power are directly connected, because the ability to speak, write, and be understood in a society's dominant language shapes access to that society's power structure (Chapter 2), while control over which languages are permitted, taught, or respected is itself an exercise of that same power. Every concept in this chapter is a specific instance of this claim.

    Suppressing and Reviving Language¶

    The most direct exercise of power over language is linguistic suppression: the deliberate prohibition or discouragement of a group's language by a dominant power, typically as part of a broader strategy of cultural suppression. Chapter 9 already documented this chapter's clearest example: the boarding school era's explicit policy of punishing Indigenous children for speaking their own languages, a policy choice — not an accident of neglect — with a single, stated goal of severing the link between a generation of children and their linguistic heritage.

    Because linguistic suppression is a documented, dated policy rather than a natural process of language loss, its effects can be, and increasingly are being, actively reversed through language revitalization: organized efforts by a community to restore, teach, and expand the use of an endangered or suppressed language, often undertaken by younger generations reconnecting with elders and documented sources. Language revitalization is a direct expression of the self-determination Chapter 9 defined — a community exercising its own authority over its own linguistic future, often against the ongoing effects of a suppression policy set in motion generations earlier.

    Everyday Language and Power: Code-Switching and Dialect Bias¶

    Power over language operates not only through formal policy but through everyday social judgment. Code-switching is the practice of shifting between languages, dialects, or registers of speech depending on social context, often practiced by bilingual or multidialectal speakers to navigate different social or professional environments. Code-switching is frequently a survival skill, not simply a linguistic curiosity — a speaker who code-switches between a home dialect and a "standard" professional register is often responding directly to the reality this chapter's next concept names.

    Dialect bias is prejudice against speakers of a nonstandard dialect or accent, in which a particular dialect (frequently the dialect associated with a dominant racial, regional, or class group) is treated as more correct, intelligent, or professional than others, despite linguists' general agreement that no dialect is inherently more grammatically valid than another. Dialect bias connects directly back to Chapter 2's stereotype-to-discrimination chain — a hiring manager who judges a job candidate's competence based on their dialect is applying prejudice (Chapter 2) through a linguistic proxy, often without recognizing bias is occurring at all.

    Names and Oral Sources¶

    Power over language extends to something as small and as significant as a name. Naming and identity describes the relationship between how a person or group is named — including the historical practice of renaming enslaved or colonized people, or the ongoing debate over Indigenous place names versus colonial replacements — and their sense of self, cultural continuity, and social recognition. The practice of renaming is not a minor historical footnote; it is directly connected to the erasure Chapter 2 defined, since a renamed person or place has had one specific, documented layer of its history and identity overwritten.

    This chapter's connection to historical method comes through oral tradition: the practice of transmitting history, values, and cultural knowledge through spoken narrative across generations, historically undervalued as a legitimate historical source by academic historiography that privileged written records, despite oral tradition's demonstrated accuracy across many documented cases. Recall Chapter 1's definition of a primary source — oral tradition, and the oral history method Chapter 14 will formalize, extends that definition to spoken, generationally transmitted accounts, a category dominant Western historiography has historically treated with unwarranted skepticism relative to written documents of comparable or lesser reliability.

    Working with any account not originally recorded in a reader's own language raises a final concern: translation bias, the distortion of meaning, tone, or nuance that occurs when a source is translated from one language to another, which can reflect the translator's own author perspective (Chapter 1) even when the translation is made in good faith. Translation bias is not necessarily intentional distortion — it can result simply from the fact that no two languages map perfectly onto each other, which means every translation involves interpretive choices a monolingual reader may never notice were made.

    Diagram: Spot the Translation Choice¶

    Spot the Translation Choice

    Type: microsim

    sim-id: spot-the-translation-choice
    Library: p5.js
    Status: Specified

    Purpose: Show students three different valid English translations of the same short non-English source excerpt, so they can see translation bias operating even without any translator acting in bad faith.

    Bloom Taxonomy: Analyze (L4) — examine differences among multiple valid translations of the same source. Bloom Verb: examine

    Learning objective: Given three professionally plausible translations of the same short excerpt, students can identify specific word or phrase choices that differ, and explain how each choice subtly shifts the excerpt's tone or emphasis without any translation being factually "wrong."

    Canvas layout: - Three side-by-side text panels, each showing a different English translation of the same short (2-3 sentence) illustrative excerpt - Below the panels, a highlight-and-compare control

    Interactive controls: - Click any word or phrase in any panel: highlights the corresponding word/phrase choice across all three panels for direct comparison - "Show Translator's Note" toggle: reveals a short explanatory note for each panel describing what interpretive choice that translation prioritized (e.g., literal word-for-word accuracy vs. capturing idiomatic tone vs. matching poetic rhythm)

    Data Visibility Requirements: Stage 1: Three panels shown side by side with no highlighting Stage 2: On word click, matching word/phrase spans highlight across all three panels Stage 3: On toggle, translator's-note text appears beneath each panel

    Instructional Rationale: An Analyze-level objective about translation bias requires direct comparison of multiple legitimate options, not a single "biased vs. unbiased" binary — showing three good-faith translations side by side demonstrates that translation bias is a structural feature of translation itself, not necessarily a sign of translator misconduct.

    Implementation: p5.js or HTML/CSS three-column layout with clickable spans pre-marked in the source text and a toggle-controlled note panel.

    Key Takeaways¶

    • Language and power connects every concept in this chapter: control over language is an exercise of power, and access to a dominant language shapes access to power.
    • Linguistic suppression and language revitalization are mirror-image, documented processes — the second is a direct, organized response to the first.
    • Code-switching is often a response to dialect bias, which applies Chapter 2's prejudice-to-discrimination chain through a linguistic proxy.
    • Naming and identity connects renaming practices to erasure; oral tradition is a historically undervalued primary source; translation bias shows author perspective operating even in good-faith translation.
