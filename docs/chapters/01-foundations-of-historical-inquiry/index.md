## Why Some Stories Get Told and Others Don't

Open any standard American history textbook to its chapter on westward expansion, and you will find pioneers, wagon trains, and a nation "fulfilling its destiny." You are less likely to find a full account of the nations displaced to make that expansion possible. Neither chapter is lying, exactly. One is simply more complete than the other. This course calls the missing half **hidden history** — the people, events, and perspectives that existed but were left out of the version of the past that got written down, taught, and repeated until it became "just history."

When a specific story, group, or perspective has been left out of that dominant record, we call it an **absent narrative**. Absent narratives are not always the result of a conspiracy. Sometimes a group's records were destroyed. Sometimes historians simply didn't think to ask. Sometimes the people who lived the story were never taught to write, or were forbidden from writing about it at all. Whatever the cause, the effect is the same: a gap in the record that later readers mistake for a gap in what happened.

Recognizing hidden history is not just an academic exercise. It requires a discipline with its own name, its own methods, and its own questions.

## Historiography: The Study of How History Is Written

**Historiography** is the study of how history itself is written — not just what happened, but who decided to record it, how they recorded it, and why. A historian practicing historiography asks questions a casual reader never thinks to ask: Who wrote this account? What did they have access to? What did they choose to leave out, and what did that choice cost the people left out of it?

Historiography treats "history" as something that is made, not something that simply exists. Every textbook, monument, and museum exhibit reflects a series of choices — about what counts as important, whose voice counts as reliable, and which stories are worth the paper (or the bronze) they're recorded on. This course asks you to notice those choices.

To notice them, you need to understand the raw material historians work with: sources.

## Primary and Secondary Sources

A **primary source** is a firsthand account or original record created by someone who witnessed or participated in an event — a diary entry, a photograph, a treaty, a recorded interview, a piece of legislation. A **secondary source** is a later account that interprets, analyzes, or summarizes primary sources — a textbook chapter, a documentary, a historian's book written decades after the fact.

Neither type of source is automatically more trustworthy than the other. A primary source can be an eyewitness's honest but incomplete memory; a secondary source can synthesize dozens of primary sources into a more accurate picture than any single witness could offer. What matters is knowing which kind of source you're looking at and what that means for how you use it.

Before we compare the two side by side, it helps to know why the distinction matters at all: primary sources put you closest to the event but carry one person's limited vantage point, while secondary sources trade that closeness for the benefit of hindsight and cross-referencing — at the cost of another layer of interpretation between you and what actually happened.

Here is how the two compare:

| Feature | Primary Source | Secondary Source |
|---|---|---|
| Created by | A witness or participant | A later researcher or writer |
| Timing | During or near the event | After the event, often much later |
| Strength | Direct, unfiltered access to the moment | Synthesis, context, and hindsight |
| Risk | Limited to one perspective | Filtered through the author's interpretation |
| Examples | Letters, photographs, government records, oral histories | Textbooks, documentaries, scholarly articles |

Every source — primary or secondary — was created by a person, and every person has a point of view.

## Source Bias and Author Perspective

**Source bias** is the tendency of any source to reflect the particular vantage point, assumptions, or interests of whoever created it. Bias does not always mean lying. A government report on a treaty negotiation may be entirely factual about the terms agreed to while still omitting how those terms were extracted under threat. The bias is in what got included and what got left as background noise.

Closely related to source bias is **author perspective**: the specific position — social, political, geographic, or personal — from which a source's creator viewed and described events. A soldier's diary and a civilian's diary describing the same battle will emphasize different details, because they experienced the event from different vantage points. Neither account is "wrong"; both are partial.

Before you can trust any conclusion drawn from a source, you need to ask questions of it. This is the habit of **critical thinking** applied to historical evidence — questioning, rather than absorbing, what a source presents.

Ask these five questions of any historical source before you use it:

- Who created this source, and what was their relationship to the event?
- When was it created — during the event, or years afterward?
- What audience was it written for, and how might that shape what's included?
- What does the source assume its reader already believes?
- What is missing that you would expect to find if the full picture were present?

A single source, no matter how carefully questioned, is rarely enough. Historians build confidence in a claim through **source corroboration** — checking whether multiple independent sources, ideally created by people with different perspectives, agree on the same basic facts. When three unrelated accounts describe the same battle the same way, historians trust the account more. When only one perspective survives, historians treat conclusions from it as provisional.

Before we look at how corroboration works in practice, let's define one more term you'll need: **historical context**, the surrounding circumstances — political, economic, social — in which an event occurred and a source was created. A source read without its historical context can be badly misread; a law that looks neutral on paper may have been designed, in its specific 1920s context, to accomplish something its plain text never states.

## Dominant Narratives, Counter-Narratives, and Historical Silence

When one version of events is repeated so often — in textbooks, monuments, films, and speeches — that it comes to feel like simply "what happened," historians call it the **dominant narrative**. A dominant narrative is not necessarily false. It is, however, always partial, because no single account can capture every perspective on a complex event. The frontier story of Manifest Destiny you will study in Chapter 4 is a dominant narrative: true in its broad strokes, and incomplete in what it leaves out.

A **counter-narrative** is an account that challenges or complicates a dominant narrative by centering the perspectives the dominant version minimized or omitted. Counter-narratives are not automatically "more true" than dominant narratives — they are subject to the same source-bias and corroboration questions as any other account. But because they draw on sources that were historically less likely to be collected, preserved, or believed, counter-narratives often require more work to recover.

That work is necessary because of **historical silence**: the absence, in the historical record, of documentation for a person, group, or event — not because nothing happened, but because no one with the power to preserve a record chose to. Historical silence is what turns an absent narrative into something genuinely difficult to research. You cannot corroborate five sources when only one was ever allowed to exist.

Historical silence is rarely random. It falls disproportionately on groups who lacked the political, economic, or educational power to create and preserve their own records — the condition this course calls **marginalization**: the process by which a group's experiences, perspectives, or needs are pushed to the edges of a society's attention, resources, or historical record. Marginalization is the throughline connecting every unit in this course, from government representation to media bias to the justice system. Recognizing it here, in its most abstract form, will let you recognize its specific forms later.

## The Reader's Responsibilities: Cultural Literacy, Self-Inquiry, and Empathy

Studying hidden history is not a passive activity. It asks something of the reader, too.

**Cultural literacy**, in the sense this course uses it, means having enough familiarity with the histories, values, and experiences of groups different from your own to recognize when a narrative is incomplete. It is built, not inherited — no one is born culturally literate, and everyone can grow more so.

Growing cultural literacy requires **self-inquiry**: the practice of examining your own assumptions, blind spots, and starting beliefs before applying critical thinking to a source. It is easy to ask "who wrote this, and why?" of a document. It is harder, and more important, to ask "what did I already believe about this topic before I opened it, and where did that belief come from?"

Self-inquiry, paired with critical thinking, creates the conditions for **perspective-taking**: the deliberate effort to understand an event or issue from a viewpoint other than your own, including viewpoints that differ from your own identity, background, or assumptions. Perspective-taking is not the same as agreement. You can fully understand why a historical actor made a choice, in their context, without endorsing that choice.

At its best, sustained perspective-taking produces **empathy in history**: an emotional as well as intellectual recognition of the human stakes behind a historical account — not sympathy from a distance, but a genuine effort to feel the weight of what a historical narrative describes. This course asks you to bring empathy to difficult material without letting that empathy replace the source-analysis skills you have just learned. The two work together: empathy without evidence becomes assumption; evidence without empathy becomes a spreadsheet.

## Key Takeaways

- **Hidden history** and **absent narratives** are not accidents of a hidden history — they are the predictable result of who has had the power to create and preserve records.
- **Historiography** studies not just what happened, but how the record of what happened was made.
- **Primary** and **secondary sources** each carry strengths and risks; neither is automatically more trustworthy.
- **Source bias**, **author perspective**, and **historical context** must be questioned before a source is trusted, and **source corroboration** builds confidence across multiple independent accounts.
- A **dominant narrative** is not necessarily false, but it is always partial; a **counter-narrative** works to recover what the dominant version omitted, often against the friction of **historical silence** produced by **marginalization**.
- Studying hidden history well requires **cultural literacy**, **self-inquiry**, **critical thinking**, **perspective-taking**, and **empathy** — together, not separately.
