# Quiz: The Vocabulary of Power and Exclusion

Test your understanding of the shared vocabulary of oppression, privilege, and representation with these review questions.

#### 1. How does the chapter define systemic oppression?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Marginalization caused by one individual acting with clear malicious intent
2. Marginalization built into a society's institutions — its laws, economy, and schools — so that it persists even when no individual is actively trying to cause harm
3. A synonym for prejudice held by a single person
4. A temporary condition that disappears once the people who created a policy have died
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Systemic oppression is marginalization built into institutions so deeply that it persists without requiring an active villain, illustrated by the chapter's example of a 1930s lending policy still shaping homeownership in 2026. Option A wrongly requires individual malice. Option C confuses a structural condition with a personal attitude. Option D is contradicted directly by the lending-policy example, which shows the effect outlasting its creators.

    **Concept Tested:** Systemic Oppression

#### 2. What is privilege, as defined in this chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. A skill a person develops through individual effort and training
2. A legal right granted only to government officials
3. An unearned advantage a person receives because of their membership in a particular group, rather than because of anything they individually did
4. A punishment imposed on a group by a power structure
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. Privilege is an unearned advantage tied to group membership rather than individual action, and the chapter notes it can be invisible to the person who holds it because a lifelong advantage is easy to mistake for how things normally work. Option A wrongly attributes privilege to effort. Option B narrows it to a legal category it is not limited to. Option D describes the opposite effect of a power structure.

    **Concept Tested:** Privilege

#### 3. What is a power structure?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The arrangement of institutions, resources, and social norms that determines which groups in a society hold authority, wealth, and influence, and which do not
2. A single law that grants authority to one branch of government
3. A building where government officials meet to pass legislation
4. A temporary alliance between two marginalized groups
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. A power structure is the pattern that emerges from many laws, institutions, and norms together, determining which groups hold authority, wealth, and influence. The chapter presents systemic oppression and privilege as two views of this same structure. Option B and C both wrongly reduce the structure to a single law or physical location, and option D describes something unrelated to the definition given.

    **Concept Tested:** Power Structure

#### 4. According to the chapter, what makes erasure different from tokenism?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Erasure only occurs in government policy, while tokenism only occurs in media
2. Erasure and tokenism describe the exact same practice
3. Erasure is a legally defined crime, while tokenism is not
4. Tokenism includes a single, symbolic member of a group without addressing the underlying power structure, while erasure removes the group's presence from the record altogether
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Tokenism is a superficial, symbolic inclusion that leaves the underlying power structure unaddressed, while erasure is the more complete failure of removing a group's presence, contributions, or experiences from a narrative or record entirely. Option A invents a restriction not stated in the text. Option B ignores the chapter's explicit contrast between the two. Option C misidentifies erasure as a legal category rather than a pattern of omission.

    **Concept Tested:** Erasure

#### 5. Which statement best explains the relationship among stereotype, prejudice, bias, and discrimination?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. They are four unrelated terms that happen to describe similar topics
2. A stereotype is a shared belief, prejudice is a resulting attitude, bias is a tendency that can be conscious or unconscious, and discrimination is the unfair action or outcome that follows
3. Discrimination always occurs first, causing the other three terms as its consequences
4. Bias and discrimination are interchangeable words for the same private belief
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. The chapter traces a chain from a shared cultural belief (stereotype) to an individual's negative attitude (prejudice) to a tendency to favor or disfavor (bias) to unfair treatment based on group membership (discrimination). Option A denies the connection the chapter builds explicitly. Option C reverses the causal order. Option D collapses bias, a tendency in judgment, into discrimination, which is action rather than belief.

    **Concept Tested:** Discrimination

#### 6. How does the chapter distinguish representation from inclusion?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Representation refers to legal quotas, while inclusion refers to voluntary charity
2. The two terms are used interchangeably throughout the chapter
3. Inclusion is a narrower concept that only applies to hiring decisions

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. Representation concerns proportionate, accurate presence and portrayal, while inclusion goes further to require that marginalized members' input actually shapes decisions, as illustrated by the chapter's example of a diverse but non-inclusive room. Option A invents categories the chapter does not use. Option B contradicts the chapter's clear distinction. Option C wrongly limits inclusion to one context.

#### 7. What is the key difference between equity and equality, as this chapter defines them?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Equality provides the same resources or treatment to everyone regardless of starting position, while equity calibrates resources or treatment to each group's specific starting position to reach a comparable outcome
2. Equity is always the ethically superior choice in any policy context
3. Equality applies only to voting rights, while equity applies only to economic policy
4. Equity means giving every group identical resources, while equality means giving groups different resources
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Equality means identical treatment regardless of starting position, while equity means treatment calibrated to each group's specific starting position with the goal of a comparable outcome. The chapter stresses that neither is automatically correct — the right choice depends on the policy's goal. Option B contradicts that neutrality. Option C invents a restriction not in the text. Option D reverses the actual definitions of the two terms.

    **Concept Tested:** Equity Vs Equality

#### 8. Why does the chapter say that anti-discrimination law can address discrimination but struggles to address prejudice directly?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Prejudice is legally protected free speech in every circumstance
2. Discrimination and prejudice are legally identical, so law addresses both equally
3. Law can act on actions and outcomes, but prejudice is a private belief, which is harder to legislate against — helping explain why systemic oppression can outlast the individual prejudices that first produced it
4. Prejudice does not exist without an accompanying discriminatory act
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter explains that discrimination is unfair treatment in action, which policy can target directly, while prejudice remains a private belief that is much harder to legislate against, a gap that helps explain why systemic oppression persists after the original prejudices fade. Option A overstates a legal protection not discussed. Option B contradicts the chapter's explicit distinction. Option D reverses the chapter's point that prejudice can exist without producing a discriminatory act.

#### 9. A city council is deciding how to distribute a limited pool of new computers among three schools with very different existing technology budgets. Applying this chapter's distinction, which approach reflects equity rather than equality?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Dividing the computers into three identical, equal-sized batches regardless of each school's current technology budget
2. Giving more computers to the schools with the smallest existing technology budgets, aiming to bring all three schools to a more comparable level of access
3. Giving all the computers to the single wealthiest school because it can maintain them most efficiently
4. Canceling the distribution entirely until every school submits an identical request form
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. This applies the chapter's equity principle: allocating resources in proportion to need, so schools with the least existing access receive more, moving all three toward a comparable outcome. Option A describes equality, an identical split that would preserve rather than close the existing gap. Option C rewards the school with the most resources already, the opposite of equity. Option D avoids the decision rather than applying either principle.

    **Concept Tested:** Equity Vs Equality

    The correct answer is **D**. The chapter's central point is that systemic oppression does not require a villain: once embedded in institutions like lending policy, it continues to shape outcomes independent of whether the original prejudiced individuals are still alive or still hold those beliefs.     **Concept Tested:** Systemic Oppression
