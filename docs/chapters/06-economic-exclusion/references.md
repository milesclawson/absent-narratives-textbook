# References: Economic Exclusion

1. [Redlining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redlining) - Wikipedia - Traces redlining from 1930s federal housing policy through its contemporary digital and environmental forms, with wealth-gap statistics tied directly to HOLC maps. Anchors the chapter's central mechanism of economic exclusion.

2. [Racial wealth gap in the United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_wealth_gap_in_the_United_States) - Wikipedia - Explains how housing, employment, education, and credit access compound over generations to produce today's wealth gap, with historical data from 1984 onward. Supports the chapter's argument about compounding economic consequences.

3. [Reparations for slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reparations_for_slavery) - Wikipedia - Surveys reparations proposals, debates, and international precedents, including quantified estimates and opposing arguments. Gives students the full range of positions the chapter asks them to evaluate.

4. The Color of Law: A Forgotten History of How Our Government Segregated America - Richard Rothstein - Liveright Publishing - A definitive account of how federal, state, and local policy deliberately created residential segregation, providing the policy history behind this chapter's redlining and housing discrimination discussion.

5. Evicted: Poverty and Profit in the American City - Matthew Desmond - Crown Publishing - A Pulitzer Prize-winning ethnography of housing instability and eviction in low-income communities, grounding the chapter's housing discrimination and community disinvestment concepts in lived experience.

6. [Mapping Inequality](https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/) - University of Richmond Digital Scholarship Lab - An interactive archive of the original 1930s HOLC redlining maps for cities nationwide, letting students examine the primary source documents behind the chapter's redlining discussion.

7. [Wealth Gaps Within Racial and Ethnic Groups](https://www.pewresearch.org/2023/12/04/wealth-gaps-within-racial-and-ethnic-groups/) - Pew Research Center - Current data on wealth disparities between Black, Hispanic, Asian, and White households, including how the gap varies by income level, updating the chapter's wealth-gap discussion with recent figures.

8. [Who Uses Legacy Admissions?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-widespread-is-the-practice-of-giving-special-consideration-to-relatives-of-alumni-in-admissions/) - Brookings Institution - Analyzes how widespread legacy admissions are across selective and public colleges and their overlap with race-conscious admissions, directly supporting the chapter's college admissions bias discussion.

9. [Black Wealth Is Increasing, But So Is the Racial Wealth Gap](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/black-wealth-is-increasing-but-so-is-the-racial-wealth-gap/) - Brookings Institution - Examines economic mobility trends alongside a persistently widening wealth gap, giving students evidence for the chapter's distinction between individual mobility and systemic exclusion.

10. [HOLC "Redlining" Maps: The Persistent Structure of Segregation and Economic Inequality](https://ncrc.org/holc/) - National Community Reinvestment Coalition - Connects historical redlining maps to present-day patterns of community disinvestment and gentrification, supporting the chapter's link between historical policy and current neighborhood change.
