## Is "Criminal" a Fact or a Label?

Ask most people what makes an act a crime, and they will describe something close to common sense: crimes are acts that harm people, and the law simply names them. This chapter asks you to examine that assumption more carefully, using the same social-construction lens Chapter 3 applied to race.

**Criminality** is the status of being classified, by a legal system, as having committed an act the system defines as a crime. Notice the wording: criminality is a classification, not an inherent property of a person or even, necessarily, of an act. The same behavior — possessing a small amount of a substance, for instance — has been legal in one U.S. state and a felony in a neighboring state in the same year. That variation is only possible if criminality is, at least in part, **socially constructed** in the sense Chapter 3 defined: built by a society's decisions, not discovered as a fixed natural fact.

This course names that specific application of social construction the **social construction of crime**: the recognition that what counts as a crime — and how seriously it is punished — reflects social and political decisions about which harms matter and which groups' behavior is treated as threatening, rather than a fixed, universal moral category. The social construction of crime does not claim that no act is genuinely harmful. It claims that the line between "criminal" and "not criminal," and the severity attached to each side of that line, is drawn by people, with all the historical biases this course has already documented.

## From Codes and Laws to Selective Enforcement

The social construction of crime becomes concrete through **codes and laws**: the formal, written rules — statutes, ordinances, regulations — through which a government defines prohibited conduct and specifies its penalties. Codes and laws are, in principle, applied equally to everyone they cover. In practice, that principle is frequently violated through **selective enforcement**: the practice of applying laws more strictly, or more frequently, to some groups than to others, even when the law's text applies equally to all.

Selective enforcement has a documented history worth tracing directly, because policing itself has changed dramatically over time. **Policing history** refers to the documented evolution of law enforcement institutions in the United States — from early slave patrols in the antebellum South, through the professionalization of municipal police in the early 20th century, to current community and militarized policing models — a history that shapes which communities police forces originated to control and which they originated to protect. Understanding policing history is not an accusation against any individual officer; it is a historical-context step, exactly as Chapter 1 defined that term, necessary before interpreting present-day enforcement patterns.

Selective enforcement does not always come from a conscious decision. **Implicit bias** — introduced briefly in Chapter 2 as a form of bias — plays a documented role here specifically: unconscious attitudes or stereotypes that affect a person's understanding, actions, and decisions without their awareness, including decisions made by police officers, prosecutors, and judges. Implicit bias helps explain how selective enforcement can occur even among officials who consciously believe they are applying the law equally.

One specific, well-studied form of selective enforcement is **racial profiling**: the practice of targeting individuals for police suspicion, stops, or searches based on their race or ethnicity rather than on individualized evidence of wrongdoing. Racial profiling has been measured directly through **prejudice statistics**: data — such as stop rates, search rates, and outcome rates broken down by race — used to measure whether selective enforcement or racial profiling is occurring within a given jurisdiction. Prejudice statistics matter because they convert a claim that could otherwise be dismissed as anecdotal into a testable, measurable pattern — exactly the kind of source corroboration Chapter 1 asked you to seek before trusting a claim.

## From Enforcement to Outcome

Selective enforcement, once it results in an arrest and conviction, does not stop shaping outcomes. **Sentencing disparity** is a measurable difference in the length or severity of criminal sentences handed down for comparable offenses, correlated with the defendant's race, class, or other demographic characteristics, even after controlling for the severity of the offense. Sentencing disparity, repeated across millions of cases over decades, is one of the primary documented drivers of **mass incarceration**: the historically and internationally unusual scale of imprisonment in the United States, both in absolute numbers and as a rate per capita, disproportionately affecting Black and Latino communities.

Before we look at the two protections meant to guard against these outcomes, consider one more concept from the justice system's own vocabulary: **due process**, the constitutional guarantee that the government must follow fair, established legal procedures before depriving a person of life, liberty, or property. Due process is the formal safeguard the justice system claims to provide against exactly the kind of unequal treatment sentencing disparity and racial profiling document — which is part of why measuring the gap between due process as a formal guarantee and enforcement as a documented practice is a central skill of this chapter.

## Consequences That Extend Beyond the Justice System

Contact with the justice system produces effects that reach beyond any individual sentence. **Criminal label impact** describes the lasting social and economic consequences a person experiences after being labeled a criminal — reduced employment prospects, housing denial, loss of voting rights in some states — which can persist long after a sentence is completed and regardless of whether the original enforcement was selective or fair. The criminal label, once applied, functions similarly to the erasure and stereotype dynamics Chapter 2 described: it can overwrite every other fact about a person with a single, sticky category.

For young people, these dynamics have a documented specific pathway: the **school-to-prison pipeline**, the pattern by which harsh school discipline policies — suspension, expulsion, on-campus policing — funnel students, disproportionately Black and Latino students and students with disabilities, out of the education system and into the juvenile or criminal justice system. The school-to-prison pipeline connects directly to **juvenile justice**: the distinct legal system and set of procedures for handling individuals under 18 accused of crimes, historically premised on rehabilitation but subject to the same documented patterns of racial disparity found in the adult system.

One historical term captures what happens when a person facing unjust or selectively enforced law seeks to escape its reach rather than submit to it: **fugitivity**, the historical and ongoing practice of evading a legal or social system perceived as illegitimate or unjustly applied, ranging from formerly enslaved people escaping captivity to contemporary forms of resistance to selective enforcement. Fugitivity is not simply "breaking the law" in the ordinary sense — this course's use of the term specifically names evasion of systems whose legitimacy the person evading has reason to question, connecting directly back to the social construction of crime this chapter opened with.

## Alternatives and Corrections

This chapter does not end with only a description of the problem. **Restorative justice** is an alternative approach to addressing crime that focuses on repairing harm, accountability, and reconciliation between the offender, the victim, and the community, rather than solely on punishment. Restorative justice is one of several concrete proposals, alongside stronger enforcement of due process protections, aimed at addressing the specific patterns of sentencing disparity, mass incarceration, and the school-to-prison pipeline this chapter has documented.

## Key Takeaways

- **Criminality** reflects the **social construction of crime** — codified into **codes and laws**, but subject to **selective enforcement**, a pattern with roots traceable through **policing history**.
- **Implicit bias** and **racial profiling** help explain how selective enforcement occurs; **prejudice statistics** provide the evidence to measure it.
- **Sentencing disparity** compounds into **mass incarceration**, despite the formal guarantee of **due process**.
- The **criminal label** carries consequences beyond a sentence, funneled disproportionately through the **school-to-prison pipeline** into the **juvenile justice** system; **fugitivity** names the historical response of evading an unjustly applied system.
- **Restorative justice** offers one documented alternative framework aimed at repairing harm rather than only punishing it.
