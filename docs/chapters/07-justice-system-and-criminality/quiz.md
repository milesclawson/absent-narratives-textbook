#### 3. What does the social construction of crime claim, according to this chapter?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. That no act is ever genuinely harmful to another person
2. That every U.S. state defines the same acts as crimes with identical penalties
3. That criminal law is entirely separate from any social or political decision-making process
4. That what counts as a crime, and how seriously it is punished, reflects social and political decisions about which harms matter and which groups' behavior is treated as threatening, rather than a fixed, universal moral category
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **D**. The social construction of crime holds that the line between criminal and not criminal, and the severity attached to each side, is drawn by people through social and political decisions rather than existing as a fixed, universal category. Option A misstates the claim, since the chapter explicitly says it does not deny real harm exists. Option B is contradicted by the chapter's own example of legal variation across states. Option C denies the very connection the concept asserts.

    **Concept Tested:** Social Construction Of Crime

#### 4. How does the chapter distinguish implicit bias from racial profiling?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The two terms describe exactly the same practice with no meaningful difference
2. Implicit bias is an unconscious attitude that can influence any decision point in the system, while racial profiling is a specific practice of targeting individuals for police suspicion based on race rather than individualized evidence
3. Racial profiling is legal in all fifty states, while implicit bias is illegal everywhere
4. Implicit bias only affects judges, while racial profiling only affects police officers
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **B**. Implicit bias is an unconscious attitude that can affect any decision point — officer, prosecutor, or judge — without the person's awareness, while racial profiling is the specific, well-studied practice of targeting individuals for stops or searches based on race rather than individualized evidence. Option A denies a distinction the chapter draws explicitly. Option C invents legal statuses not discussed in the text. Option D wrongly restricts both concepts to a single role each.

    **Concept Tested:** Racial Profiling
