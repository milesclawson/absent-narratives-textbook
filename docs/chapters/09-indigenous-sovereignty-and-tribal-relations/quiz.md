#### 1. How does this chapter define Indigenous sovereignty?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. The inherent, pre-existing right of Indigenous nations to govern their own citizens, territory, laws, and cultural practices, a right that predates the United States and is only recognized, not granted, by it
2. A right created by the U.S. Congress in the 1970s
3. A status that applies only to nations that have signed a treaty in the last fifty years
4. A ceremonial title with no legal significance
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **A**. Indigenous sovereignty is the inherent, pre-existing right of Indigenous nations to govern themselves, predating the United States and only recognized or violated, never granted, by it — a distinction that determines whether tribal authority can be legitimately taken away by Congress. Option B reverses this origin, treating a pre-existing right as a federal creation. Option C invents a time restriction not in the text. Option D contradicts the chapter's emphasis on real legal rights, such as treaty rights.

    **Concept Tested:** Indigenous Sovereignty

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
1. Preparing Indigenous students for careers in tribal government

3. Cultural elimination through forced assimilation, achieved by separating children from their families, languages, and cultural practices
4. Providing bilingual education in both English and Indigenous languages
</div>

??? question "Show Answer"
    The correct answer is **C**. The chapter states plainly that the boarding school era's explicit, documented policy goal was cultural elimination, not education in the ordinary sense, achieved by forcibly separating Indigenous children from their families, languages, and cultural practices. #### 4. What does the chapter say about the origin of blood quantum as a concept?

<div class="upper-alpha" markdown>
