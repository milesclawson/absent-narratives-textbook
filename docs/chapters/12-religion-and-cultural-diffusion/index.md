## Studying Religion as History, Not Theology

This chapter studies religion the way the rest of this course studies government, media, and migration: as a historical and social phenomenon shaped by diffusion, power, and context — not as a matter of theological truth or personal belief. Nothing in this chapter asks you to evaluate which religious tradition is correct. It asks you to analyze how religious traditions have developed, spread, and interacted with political power, using the cultural diffusion framework Chapter 10 introduced.

## Traditions Often Underrepresented in Standard Curricula

Many world-history courses move quickly past Indigenous spiritual traditions on the way to what they treat as the "major" world religions. This course begins with them deliberately. **Shamanism** is a spiritual practice, found across many Indigenous cultures worldwide, in which a designated practitioner (a shaman) is understood to mediate between the human and spirit worlds, often through ritual, trance, or healing practices, to address a community's spiritual and physical needs. Closely related, **animism** is the belief, foundational to many Indigenous spiritual traditions, that natural objects, places, and phenomena — animals, rivers, mountains — possess a spirit or life force deserving of respect and relationship.

Shamanism and animism are frequently mischaracterized in mainstream media and older textbooks as primitive precursors to "real" religion — a framing this course rejects. That framing is itself an instance of the ethnocentric bias this course has spent eleven chapters teaching you to recognize: treating one's own culturally dominant tradition as the standard against which others are measured and found lacking, precisely the same structure Chapter 3 identified in the beauty standard.

## Traditions Originating in South and East Asia

**Hinduism** is one of the world's oldest continuously practiced religious traditions, originating in the Indian subcontinent, encompassing a diverse range of beliefs, deities, and practices unified less by a single doctrine than by shared texts, cultural continuity, and philosophical traditions developed over more than three thousand years. Hinduism's internal diversity is itself instructive: a religion practiced by over a billion people today does not reduce neatly to a single, simple description, a caution worth applying to every tradition in this chapter.

Emerging from within that same religious and philosophical context roughly 2,500 years ago, **Buddhism** is a religious and philosophical tradition, founded by Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha) in the Indian subcontinent, centered on the Four Noble Truths and the pursuit of liberation from suffering, which subsequently spread across Asia through the cultural diffusion mechanisms Chapter 10 described. Buddhism's spread across Asia — from South Asia into East Asia, Southeast Asia, and beyond — is one of the best-documented historical examples of both expansion and relocation diffusion operating together over many centuries.

## Traditions Originating in the Middle East

Three related traditions, historically connected through shared textual and prophetic lineage, originated in the Middle East. **Judaism** is the religious tradition and cultural identity of the Jewish people, tracing its origins to the ancient Israelites and centered on the Torah, ethical monotheism, and a covenantal relationship between the Jewish people and God, along with a distinct historical experience of diaspora (Chapter 10) spanning millennia. **Christianity** is a religious tradition, emerging from within first-century Judaism, centered on the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth, understood by adherents as the Messiah, which subsequently spread globally and diversified into numerous denominations. **Islam** is a religious tradition, founded in seventh-century Arabia through the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad, centered on the Quran and the Five Pillars, sharing key figures and narrative traditions with Judaism and Christianity while maintaining distinct theology and practice.

Before comparing these three traditions, it is worth naming the historical connection precisely: Judaism, Christianity, and Islam are often grouped as "Abrahamic" traditions because each traces spiritual lineage to the figure of Abraham, and each developed, in part, in direct historical relationship — sometimes cooperative, sometimes conflictual — with the other two, over more than a millennium of shared Middle Eastern and Mediterranean history.

## A Tradition Born of Diaspora Itself

One tradition in this chapter emerged not from a single founding figure or region, but directly from the forced migration Chapter 10 described. **African diasporic religion** refers to religious traditions developed by enslaved Africans and their descendants throughout the Americas, blending West and Central African spiritual practices with elements of Christianity and Indigenous American traditions encountered through forced migration and diaspora — examples include Vodou, Santería, and Candomblé, each distinct and rooted in specific African source cultures and specific regions of the diaspora. African diasporic religion is a direct, documented instance of the relocation diffusion and cultural diffusion Chapter 10 defined, occurring under the most extreme and coercive circumstances covered anywhere in this course.

## Religion and Power

Every tradition examined in this chapter has, at some point in its history, both suffered and been used to justify **religious persecution**: the mistreatment, discrimination, or violence directed at individuals or groups because of their religious beliefs or practices, carried out by a dominant religious or political power against a minority tradition. Religious persecution is not unique to any single tradition or era — it recurs across the historical record whenever religious identity intersects with political power, from the persecution of early Christians under Rome, to European antisemitism culminating in the Holocaust, to the forced suppression of Indigenous spiritual practices (connecting directly to the boarding school era's cultural suppression, Chapter 9), to contemporary discrimination against Muslim, Jewish, Hindu, Buddhist, and other religious minorities in various countries including the United States.

The documented alternative to religious persecution is **religious pluralism**: a societal condition in which multiple religious traditions coexist with legal protection and mutual social tolerance, rather than one tradition holding exclusive legal or social dominance. Religious pluralism is a specific, achievable, and historically documented social arrangement — not simply the absence of persecution, but the active legal and cultural protection of multiple traditions simultaneously.

## Key Takeaways

- This chapter studies religion historically and sociologically, not theologically — no tradition is presented as more true than another.
- **Shamanism** and **animism**, common to many Indigenous traditions, are often mischaracterized in mainstream curricula — an instance of the same ethnocentric bias this course has taught you to recognize elsewhere.
- **Hinduism** and **Buddhism** share South Asian origins and illustrate cultural diffusion across Asia over millennia.
- **Judaism**, **Christianity**, and **Islam** share a documented historical lineage while remaining theologically distinct traditions.
- **African diasporic religion** developed directly from forced migration and diaspora, blending African, Christian, and Indigenous American elements under coercive historical conditions.
- **Religious persecution** recurs across traditions and eras whenever religious identity intersects with political power; **religious pluralism** is the documented, achievable alternative.
