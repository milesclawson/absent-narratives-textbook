# References: Language and Power

1. [Code-switching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code-switching) - Wikipedia - Explains the sociolinguistic theory behind shifting between languages or dialects depending on social context, including its effects on student identity and classroom power dynamics. Directly supports the chapter's code-switching concept.

2. [Language revitalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_revitalization) - Wikipedia - Surveys methods and case studies for reviving endangered languages, including the successful revival of Hebrew and ongoing Indigenous language efforts worldwide. Anchors the chapter's language revitalization concept.

3. [Oral tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition) - Wikipedia - Examines the historiographical methods scholars use to treat oral sources as legitimate historical evidence, including the influential work of Jan Vansina. Supports the chapter's treatment of oral tradition as a historical source.

4. Talkin and Testifyin: The Language of Black America - Geneva Smitherman - Wayne State University Press - A foundational linguistic study of African American English as a rule-governed dialect rather than "broken" English, directly supporting the chapter's dialect bias concept.

5. Talking Back, Talking Black: Truths About America's Lingua Franca - John McWhorter - Bellevue Literary Press - A linguist's accessible defense of African American English against common misconceptions, giving students a contemporary complement to the chapter's dialect bias and code-switching discussion.

6. [World Languages: Diversity, Endangerment, and Revitalization](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/education/teaching-resources/anthropology-and-social-studies/world-languages-diversity-endangerment-and-revitalization) - Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History - A classroom resource explaining why languages disappear and how communities work to revive them, extending the chapter's language revitalization concept with teaching materials.

7. [Born in Slavery: Slave Narratives from the Federal Writers' Project](https://www.loc.gov/collections/slave-narratives-from-the-federal-writers-project-1936-to-1938/about-this-collection/) - Library of Congress - A digitized collection of over 2,300 first-person oral history interviews with formerly enslaved people, giving students a primary-source example of oral tradition functioning as historical evidence.

8. [Language Vitality Initiative](https://folklife.si.edu/language-vitality-initiative) - Smithsonian Center for Folklife and Cultural Heritage - Documents ongoing projects to sustain minoritized languages worldwide, supporting the chapter's connection between linguistic suppression and active revitalization efforts.

9. [The Sound of Racial Profiling: When Language Leads to Discrimination](https://www.unr.edu/nevada-today/blogs/2020/the-sound-of-racial-profiling) - University of Nevada, Reno - Explains linguistic research on how accent and dialect trigger discrimination in housing, employment, and courtrooms, directly supporting the chapter's dialect bias concept with case evidence.

10. [Recovering Voices](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/research/anthropology/programs/recovering-voices) - Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History - A partnership program documenting and revitalizing endangered Indigenous languages and the knowledge embedded in them, connecting the chapter's language revitalization and naming-and-identity concepts to ongoing community work.
