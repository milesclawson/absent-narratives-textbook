## Starting With a Question

Every research project — professional or student — begins with a **research question**: a clear, focused, and answerable question that defines the scope and purpose of a research investigation, specific enough to guide source-gathering but open enough to allow the evidence to lead somewhere the researcher did not already assume. A research question about an absent narrative might ask, for example, what role a specific overlooked group played in a well-documented local event, or why a specific community's account of an event differs from the standard textbook version.

A strong research question for this course's capstone shares three qualities: it names a specific place, group, or event (not "racism in history," but a specific documented instance); it can plausibly be answered with sources a high school researcher can actually access; and it connects to at least one of this course's five units (government, economics, justice, media) or its case studies (Manifest Destiny, Indigenous sovereignty, migration, civil rights, religion, language).

## Finding the Sources

With a research question set, two complementary research methods let you gather evidence. **Archival research** is the systematic search for and analysis of primary source documents held in archives — libraries, historical societies, government records offices, digitized online collections — to answer a research question. Archival research extends Chapter 1's primary-source definition into a practical skill: knowing where such sources are held and how to search for them.

Where archival research recovers written and recorded documents, the **oral history method** is the systematic practice of collecting, recording, and preserving firsthand spoken accounts of historical events from people who experienced them, treating those accounts as primary sources with their own standards of rigor. The oral history method is this chapter's direct application of Chapter 13's oral tradition concept — it is how you formally recover the kind of source dominant historiography has historically undervalued.

Conducting oral history well requires **interview technique**: the specific skills of preparing thoughtful open-ended questions, listening actively, avoiding leading questions that suggest a desired answer, and creating a comfortable environment for a narrator to share difficult or personal history. Before conducting any interview for this course's capstone, review this checklist:

- Prepare open-ended questions in advance, but be willing to follow where the narrator's answer leads rather than sticking rigidly to a script
- Avoid leading questions ("Wasn't that unfair?") that suggest the answer you expect or want
- Ask for permission to record, and explain clearly how the recording and any resulting written account will be used
- Allow silence — a pause after a question is often a narrator gathering their thoughts on a difficult memory, not a signal to move on
- Thank the narrator and offer to share the finished project with them before it is published or presented

Once archival and oral sources are gathered, apply **source triangulation** — an extension of Chapter 1's source corroboration — the practice of checking a claim against three or more independent sources of different types (for example, a government record, a newspaper account, and an oral history interview), specifically to see whether different source types converge on the same account or reveal a gap. Source triangulation across different source types is more rigorous than corroboration across similar sources, because it tests whether a claim holds up when viewed from genuinely different vantage points, not just multiple accounts from a similar perspective.

## Researching Responsibly

Recovering an absent narrative carries a specific responsibility this course has flagged since Chapter 1: **ethics of representation** — the ethical obligations a researcher has toward the people and communities whose stories they are telling, including obtaining informed consent, avoiding exploitation, respecting a community's own preferences about how their story is told, and not claiming a voice that is not the researcher's own to claim. The ethics of representation is not a bureaucratic formality — it is the difference between recovering a marginalized voice and committing a new act of appropriation in the recovering.

Applying the ethics of representation, along with everything gathered through archival research, oral history, and triangulation, produces **narrative reconstruction**: the process of assembling verified sources into a coherent historical account that restores a previously absent or marginalized perspective, explicitly citing the evidence supporting each claim. Narrative reconstruction is the capstone skill this entire course has been building toward — every earlier chapter's case study modeled this process; now you perform it yourself.

## Choosing a Format: Documentary, Exhibit, Policy, or Action

A reconstructed narrative needs a form. This course offers four documented, legitimate formats, corresponding to the "Create" level of the course's own Bloom's Taxonomy learning outcomes.

A **historical documentary** presents a reconstructed narrative through recorded video or audio, combining narration, interview footage, and primary-source images or documents into a structured account intended for a public audience. An **exhibit design** presents a reconstructed narrative through a physical or digital display — panels, artifacts, images, captions — organized to guide a visitor through the narrative's key evidence and argument, following principles of **public history**: the practice of presenting historical research to non-academic public audiences (museums, community events, local history days) in accessible, engaging formats without sacrificing accuracy.

Two additional formats connect directly back to earlier chapters. A **policy proposal** applies a reconstructed narrative's findings to recommend a specific, concrete government or institutional policy change — connecting directly to Chapter 5's legislative change and Chapter 11's social movement strategy content. A **community action** applies a reconstructed narrative's findings to organize a specific, concrete local action — a commemoration event, an educational campaign, a partnership with a local historical society — connecting directly to Chapter 11's grassroots organizing.

Here is a summary to help you select a format for your own capstone:

| Format | Best Suited For | Connects Most Directly To |
|---|---|---|
| Historical documentary | Narratives with strong oral history or visual source material | Chapter 1 (source analysis), Chapter 13 (oral tradition) |
| Exhibit design | Narratives with strong artifact or image evidence | Chapter 9 (repatriation, Indigenous iconography) |
| Policy proposal | Narratives connected to an ongoing, addressable policy issue | Chapter 5, Chapter 6 (government and economic exclusion) |
| Community action | Narratives with a living, present-day community stake | Chapter 11 (grassroots organizing, contemporary activism) |

All four formats are legitimate expressions of the same underlying work: a properly sourced, ethically conducted **capstone project** — the culminating research project of this course, in which a student selects a research question, conducts archival and/or oral history research, applies source triangulation and the ethics of representation, and produces a narrative reconstruction in one of the formats above.

## Finishing the Work: Review, Citation, and Presentation

A capstone project is not complete when the research ends — it requires the same scholarly rigor this course has modeled throughout. **Peer review** is the practice of having classmates or other qualified reviewers examine a research project for accuracy, fairness, source quality, and adherence to the ethics of representation before it is finalized or presented, catching errors and blind spots the original researcher may not see in their own work. Peer review connects directly back to Chapter 1's emphasis on self-inquiry — a peer reviewer can catch an assumption or blind spot the original researcher's own self-inquiry missed.

Every claim in a finished capstone project must be traceable through careful **citation practice**: the consistent, accurate documentation of every source used in a research project, allowing any reader to verify a claim or locate the original source themselves. Citation practice is the final, formal expression of source corroboration and source triangulation — a claim without a citation is a claim a future researcher cannot verify, which is exactly the condition that produced historical silence in the first place.

The course's final skill is **presenting findings**: the practice of communicating a research project's methods, evidence, and conclusions clearly to an audience, whether through spoken presentation, written report, or the documentary, exhibit, policy proposal, or community action format the project took. Presenting findings closes the loop this course opened in Chapter 1: a hidden history, once recovered through rigorous method and presented clearly to an audience, is hidden no longer.
