## Title

Absent Narratives: A Comprehensive Study of Marginalized Voices in History

## Course Overview

An 18-week (one semester) high school elective asking students to critically
consider the impact of race, ethnicity, nationality, gender, and sexuality on
the course of human history. Students question, investigate, interpret, and
reflect on the stories of those whose life experiences have been marginalized
because of these characteristics and identities, and build cultural literacy
through self-inquiry and exploration for a more complete understanding of the
world and its living history. Hidden history — the untold, overlooked, and
intentionally obscured stories of marginalized groups, suppressed events, and
alternative perspectives — is studied not for recall of facts alone, but to
build global and multicultural relationships, teach empathy across
backgrounds, and help every student see their own story reflected in the
curriculum. The course fulfills an additional pathway toward Minnesota's
ethnic studies course requirement.

## Audience

High school students in grades 10–12 who are curious about the stories left
out of traditional textbooks, willing to do tedious hands-on primary- and
secondary-source research, and self-motivated. No prior background in
historiography is assumed, but students are expected to arrive with the
foundational civic, geographic, and identity-focused analytical skills built
in 9th grade Human Geography and Ethnic Studies.

## Prerequisites

- 9th grade Human Geography and Ethnic Studies (or AP Human Geography)
- Basic civic-literacy skills: reading primary and secondary sources,
  identifying an author's perspective, comparing accounts of the same event

## Topics

1. What is Hidden History — vocabulary and the study of absent narratives
2. Historiography — who writes history, whose stories are excluded, and why
3. Primary and secondary source analysis for uncovering absent narratives
4. Case study: Manifest Destiny and the narratives it obscured
5. Government representation: systemic racism and sexism in policy
6. Gerrymandering and its effects on political representation
7. Voter intimidation and suppression, historical and contemporary
8. Economic exclusion: embargoes and their disproportionate impacts
9. Redlining and housing discrimination
10. Bias in college admissions
11. The construction of criminality: what is a criminal, and who decides
12. Codes, laws, and the justice system's differential treatment of groups
13. Statistics on prejudice within the justice system
14. Representation in media: colorism and dominant beauty standards
15. Bias in news headlines and framing
16. The social construction of race and its use to justify oppression
17. Indigenous sovereignty and treaty relationships with the U.S. and Minnesota
18. Migration, forced displacement, and diaspora
19. Liberation and civil rights movements, past and present
20. Language and power: how language has been used for and against marginalized groups

## Topics Not Covered

To keep the course focused within one semester, the following related topics
are intentionally out of scope (some are addressed in the 9th grade Human
Geography and Ethnic Studies prerequisite, others belong to other courses):

- General world-history survey content already covered in prerequisite courses
- In-depth economic theory beyond the specific case studies of embargoes,
  redlining, and college admissions
- Legal training in constitutional or case law beyond what is needed to
  discuss codes, statutes, and their differential enforcement
- International comparative government systems outside the U.S./Minnesota
  context, except where directly relevant to colonialism or migration
- Clinical or psychological treatments of trauma; the course addresses
  historical and social analysis, not counseling or therapeutic practice

## Learning Outcomes

After this course, students will be able to:

- **Remember:** Recall key vocabulary of hidden history and historiography;
  identify major events and case studies (e.g., Manifest Destiny, the U.S.
  civil rights movement) covered in the course; list the categories of
  identity (race, ethnicity, nationality, gender, sexuality) central to the
  course's analytical frame.
- **Understand:** Explain why certain narratives have been excluded or
  minimized from mainstream history; describe how systemic racism, sexism,
  and other structural forces operate in government, economics, and the
  justice system; summarize the social construction of race and its
  historical uses.
- **Apply:** Use primary and secondary source analysis methods to
  investigate an absent narrative of the student's choosing; apply
  historiographical questions (who wrote this, for whom, and why) to a
  historical text or media artifact.
- **Analyze:** Analyze the role of race, ethnicity, nationality, gender, and
  sexuality in shaping historical and contemporary events; examine bias in
  media representation, headlines, and beauty standards; compare how
  different groups have experienced exclusion from dominant narratives.
- **Evaluate:** Evaluate the reliability, perspective, and completeness of
  historical sources; assess the impact of policies such as redlining,
  gerrymandering, and voter suppression on marginalized communities;
  critique how criminality and prejudice are constructed and measured.
- **Create:** Research and reconstruct a previously hidden or marginalized
  historical narrative using primary and secondary sources, presenting it in
  a way that restores the voice and perspective of the people involved;
  design an original multimedia or written presentation (e.g., podcast,
  exhibit, essay, or documentary) that makes an absent narrative accessible
  to a broader audience; propose a policy or community action that responds
  to a historical pattern of exclusion identified through the student's own
  research.
