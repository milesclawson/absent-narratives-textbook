    Glossary of Terms¶

    Ableism¶

    Discrimination or social bias against people with disabilities, based on the assumption that typical physical or mental ability is the standard.

    Ableism shaped laws that once excluded disabled people from public buildings, schools, and employment before later rights reforms.

    Example: Buildings constructed without ramps or elevators reflected an ableist assumption that only non-disabled people would need access.

    Absent Narrative¶

    A story or viewpoint missing from a historical record because the people who lived it lacked the power, literacy, or access to document and preserve it.

    Absent narratives differ from hidden history in that the account may never have been written down at all, not merely suppressed.

    Example: Enslaved people's own firsthand accounts of daily life are largely absent narratives, since most were legally barred from learning to write.

    Access To Credit¶

    The degree to which a person or community can obtain loans or financing from banks and other lenders.

    Unequal access to credit, shaped by practices like redlining, has been a major driver of the racial wealth gap.

    African Diasporic Religion¶

    Religious traditions developed by African peoples and their descendants dispersed through forced migration, often blending African spiritual practices with elements of other religions encountered abroad.

    African diasporic religion illustrates how enslaved and displaced communities preserved and adapted spiritual practices despite cultural suppression.

    Example: Vodou in Haiti developed by blending West African spiritual traditions with Catholicism under conditions of slavery and colonization.

    Algorithmic Bias¶

    A systematic pattern of unfair outcomes produced by a computer algorithm, often because it was trained on data that reflected existing social bias.

    Algorithmic bias is an emerging concern in areas from facial recognition to hiring software, extending historical patterns of discrimination into new technology.

    Example: A facial recognition system misidentifying people with darker skin tones at higher rates than lighter-skinned people demonstrates algorithmic bias.

    American Exceptionalism¶

    The belief that the United States holds a unique and superior role in history compared to other nations, often used to justify its policies.

    American exceptionalism can obscure critical examination of episodes such as Indigenous displacement or slavery within national history.

    Animism¶

    A belief system holding that natural objects, places, and phenomena, such as rivers or animals, possess a spirit or life force.

    Animism appears in many Indigenous belief systems and is studied here as a historical worldview shaping how communities related to land.

    Example: Traditional Ojibwe beliefs describing spirits present in rivers, animals, and forests reflect an animist worldview.

    Anti-Blackness¶

    Prejudice, discrimination, and systemic disadvantage specifically directed against Black people, rooted in centuries of slavery and segregation.

    Anti-Blackness is studied as a distinct historical phenomenon because it has shaped specific U.S. laws and institutions from slavery through the present.

    Archival Research¶

    The process of locating and examining original historical documents, records, or artifacts preserved in archives, libraries, or museum collections.

    Archival research is often the primary method for recovering absent narratives, since many marginalized groups' records survive only in scattered collections.

    Example: A student examining original Freedmen's Bureau records held in a national archive is conducting archival research.

    Author Perspective¶

    The position, background, and set of experiences that shape how a writer selects, frames, and interprets the information in a source.

    Identifying author perspective helps students separate factual content from the lens through which it was presented.

    Example: A missionary's journal and an Indigenous elder's oral account of the same encounter reflect very different author perspectives.

    Beauty Standard¶

    A culturally dominant ideal of physical attractiveness that a society treats as the norm against which people are judged.

    Beauty standards rooted in one group's features can marginalize people who do not match them, especially in media.

    Example: Mid-twentieth-century American advertising overwhelmingly featured straight hair and light skin as the beauty standard.

    Beauty Standard Bias¶

    The favoring of one narrow beauty standard across media and advertising, which marginalizes people whose appearance does not match it.

    Beauty standard bias intersects with colorism and can shape a young audience's sense of self-worth.

    Bias¶

    A tendency to favor or disfavor a person, group, or idea that affects judgment, often without the person recognizing it.

    Bias can appear in sources, institutions, and individuals, and this course asks students to identify it across all three.

    Blood Quantum¶

    A system, imposed by the U.S. government, that measures a person's Indigenous ancestry by fraction to determine tribal membership eligibility.

    Blood quantum is controversial because many tribes now set their own membership criteria, which do not always rely on this federally imposed measure.

    Example: A federal-era policy requiring one-quarter documented ancestry for enrollment is a blood quantum requirement still used by some nations today.

    Boarding School Era¶

    The period from the late 1800s through the mid-1900s when the U.S. and Canadian governments forcibly placed Indigenous children in residential schools designed to eliminate their culture and language.

    The boarding school era is a documented instance of forced cultural change carried out through federal education policy.

    Example: The Carlisle Indian Industrial School, opened in Pennsylvania in 1879, became a model for schools that cut children's hair and forbade native languages.

    Border Formation¶

    The historical process by which national boundaries are established, often through treaty, conquest, or colonial negotiation without regard to the people living there.

    Border formation has frequently divided existing communities, including Indigenous nations, without their consent.

    Example: The border drawn between the United States and Mexico after the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo split existing communities and Indigenous lands.

    Buddhism¶

    A religious and philosophical tradition originating in ancient India, founded on the teachings attributed to Siddhartha Gautama, focused on ending suffering through spiritual practice.

    Buddhism's spread across Asia is studied here as an example of cultural diffusion carried along trade and migration routes.

    Example: Buddhist monasteries spread from India along trade routes into China, Korea, and Japan over the first millennium CE.

    Capstone Project¶

    A culminating research assignment that requires synthesizing skills and knowledge from an entire course into an original, in-depth piece of work.

    The capstone project in this course typically applies methods such as archival research and oral history to recover an absent narrative.

    Christianity¶

    A religious tradition centered on the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth, which became a dominant institutional and cultural force across much of world history.

    This course examines Christianity's historical role both as a majority tradition shaping law and culture and as a factor in colonization and cultural suppression.

    Example: Spanish colonial missions established across the Americas combined religious conversion to Christianity with colonial control.

    Citation Practice¶

    The consistent method of crediting the original source of information, ideas, or quotations used in research writing.

    Careful citation practice allows readers to verify claims and trace an argument back to its original evidence.

    Example: Crediting a specific archival collection and page number when quoting a historical letter reflects sound citation practice.

    Civic Participation¶

    Involvement in the public life of a community or nation, including voting, activism, public testimony, and civic organizations.

    Barriers to civic participation, such as voter suppression, historically limited marginalized groups' influence over the laws governing them.

    Civil Disobedience¶

    The deliberate, public, and nonviolent refusal to obey a law considered unjust, often accepting arrest as part of the protest.

    Civil disobedience is a specific tactic within the broader strategy of nonviolent resistance.

    Example: Rosa Parks's refusal to give up her bus seat in 1955 is a well-known act of civil disobedience.

    Civil Rights Movement¶

    The mid-twentieth-century U.S. movement, led primarily by Black Americans, that used organizing, legal challenges, and nonviolent protest to end legal segregation and secure equal rights.

    The Civil Rights Movement produced landmark legislation, including the Voting Rights Act, and remains a model referenced by later social movements.

    Example: The 1955-1956 Montgomery Bus Boycott, sparked by Rosa Parks's arrest, was a pivotal early campaign of the Civil Rights Movement.

    Classism¶

    Prejudice or discrimination against people based on their perceived social or economic class, often favoring wealthier groups over poorer ones.

    Classism can intersect with race and gender to compound economic exclusion across generations.

    Example: Historical property requirements for voting excluded poor citizens regardless of race, an explicit form of classism.

    Code-Switching¶

    The practice of shifting between languages, dialects, or speech styles depending on the social context or audience a speaker is addressing.

    Code-switching is often adopted by members of marginalized groups navigating between their home community and dominant institutional settings.

    Example: A bilingual student speaking Spanish at home and formal English at school is practicing code-switching.

    Codes And Laws¶

    The formal written rules a government enacts to regulate behavior and specify punishments for violations.

    Codes and laws are the mechanism through which social attitudes about criminality become enforceable and binding.

    College Admissions Bias¶

    Systematic advantages or disadvantages in university admissions decisions tied to an applicant's race, class, or family connections rather than individual qualification alone.

    College admissions bias has been documented and debated through practices such as legacy preferences and unequal test preparation resources.

    Example: Standardized tests that correlate with a family's ability to afford private tutoring can introduce college admissions bias.

    Colonialism¶

    The policy or practice of a country extending control over a foreign territory, typically by establishing settlements and exploiting the territory's resources and people.

    Colonialism provides essential background for understanding land dispossession, forced migration, and the origins of many present-day national borders.

    Example: British colonial rule over India from the eighteenth through twentieth centuries extracted resources and restructured local governance.

    Colorism¶

    Prejudice or preferential treatment based on the relative lightness or darkness of a person's skin, often occurring within the same racial or ethnic group.

    Colorism shows that discrimination can operate along gradations of appearance, not only along broad racial categories.

    Example: Historical hiring practices in some industries favored lighter-skinned Black applicants over darker-skinned applicants, a documented colorism pattern.

    Colorism In Media¶

    The tendency of film, television, and advertising to favor lighter-skinned actors and models over darker-skinned members of the same racial or ethnic group.

    Colorism in media can shape beauty standards and limit opportunities for darker-skinned performers.

    Example: Casting predominantly light-skinned actors in leading roles while relegating darker-skinned actors to minor parts reflects colorism in media.

    Community Action¶

    An organized effort by local residents to address a shared problem or advocate for change within their own community.

    Community action connects historical study of grassroots organizing to present-day opportunities for civic engagement.

    Example: Neighborhood residents petitioning a city council for a new community health clinic are engaging in community action.

    Community Disinvestment¶

    The withdrawal or withholding of public and private investment, such as loans, infrastructure spending, or business development, from a particular neighborhood or region.

    Community disinvestment often followed discriminatory lending practices, leaving affected neighborhoods with fewer resources for decades afterward.

    Example: A formerly redlined neighborhood that received little bank lending or municipal infrastructure spending for decades illustrates community disinvestment.

    Contemporary Activism¶

    Present-day organized efforts to advance social or political change, often building on strategies and goals from earlier historical movements.

    Contemporary activism allows students to connect historical movements studied in this course to ongoing debates and organizing today.

    Example: Recent organizing around voting access legislation echoes strategies first used during the civil rights-era campaign for the Voting Rights Act.

    Contemporary Indigenous Voice¶

    The present-day perspectives, art, journalism, and scholarship produced by Indigenous people, offering a counterpoint to historical accounts written by outsiders.

    Centering contemporary Indigenous voice corrects the tendency of older texts to describe Indigenous peoples only as a historical subject, not as living communities.

    Example: A tribal newspaper reporting on current reservation policy debates represents contemporary Indigenous voice.

    Counter-Narrative¶

    An account that challenges or complicates a dominant narrative by presenting evidence and perspectives the dominant version omitted or misrepresented.

    Counter-narratives are a central tool of this course for restoring marginalized voices to the historical record.

    Example: Formerly enslaved people's memoirs offered a counter-narrative to slaveholders' claims that enslaved people were content with their condition.

    Counter-Storytelling¶

    The practice of telling a story from a marginalized perspective to challenge and complicate a dominant narrative about an event or group.

    Counter-storytelling is a scholarly method as well as a narrative technique, used throughout this course to recover absent narratives.

    Example: A documentary centered on Indigenous survivors' own accounts of a historical policy is a form of counter-storytelling.

    Criminal Label Impact¶

    The lasting social and economic consequences a person faces after being labeled a criminal, including barriers to employment, housing, and voting.

    The criminal label impact can persist long after a sentence is served, compounding the effects of mass incarceration.

    Example: A former offender being denied a job years after completing their sentence, because of a background check, shows criminal label impact.

    Criminality¶

    The social and legal status assigned to behavior a society defines as criminal, which can shift over time and differ across groups.

    This course examines criminality as a socially defined category rather than a fixed, objective one, since laws defining crime have changed dramatically over time.

    Critical Thinking¶

    The disciplined evaluation of evidence, arguments, and sources to reach a reasoned conclusion rather than accepting claims at face value.

    This course applies critical thinking to weigh competing historical narratives against the available documentary evidence.

    Cultural Diffusion¶

    The spread of cultural beliefs, customs, technologies, or practices from one society to another through contact, trade, or migration.

    Cultural diffusion can occur voluntarily through exchange or be imposed through conquest and colonization.

    Example: The spread of the Spanish language across Latin America following colonization resulted from cultural diffusion under colonial rule.

    Cultural Identity¶

    The sense of belonging a person derives from shared customs, language, values, and history with a particular cultural group.

    Policies aimed at eliminating a group's customs and language, such as those imposed on Indigenous children, directly targeted cultural identity.

    Cultural Literacy¶

    The knowledge and understanding needed to recognize and interpret the customs, values, and historical experiences of a culture different from one's own.

    Building cultural literacy helps students evaluate sources and narratives about groups whose experiences differ from their own.

    Cultural Suppression¶

    Government or institutional efforts to eliminate a group's language, religion, customs, or traditions, often through law or forced assimilation.

    Cultural suppression was a defining feature of the boarding school era but also appears in other historical contexts, such as banning minority languages.

    Example: Punishing Indigenous children for speaking their native language in boarding schools was a deliberate act of cultural suppression.

    Desegregation¶

    The process of ending the legally enforced separation of racial groups in schools, public facilities, and other institutions.

    Desegregation was mandated by the 1954 Supreme Court case Brown v. Board of Education, though implementation faced significant resistance for years afterward.

    Example: Nine Black students, known as the Little Rock Nine, desegregated Central High School in Arkansas in 1957 under federal protection.

    Dialect Bias¶

    Prejudice against speakers of a nonstandard dialect, based on the assumption that their speech pattern reflects lower intelligence or education.

    Dialect bias can affect how a person is judged in schools, workplaces, and media regardless of the actual content of what they say.

    Example: Judging a job applicant as less competent solely because they speak a regional or minority dialect reflects dialect bias.

    Diaspora¶

    A population of people living outside their ancestral homeland, often while maintaining cultural, religious, or emotional ties to that origin.

    Diaspora communities frequently preserve cultural identity and historical memory even generations after their ancestors' original displacement.

    Example: The African diaspora refers to the global communities descended from Africans dispersed through the slave trade and later migration.

    Diffusion Model¶

    A framework historians and social scientists use to trace and explain how a cultural practice, technology, or idea moved between societies over time.

    A diffusion model helps distinguish between voluntary cultural exchange and diffusion imposed through conquest or unequal power relationships.

    Digital Media Bias¶

    A systematic slant in how online platforms, search engines, and social media present or prioritize certain content, groups, or viewpoints over others.

    Digital media bias is harder to detect than traditional print bias because algorithms, not human editors, often determine what content spreads.

    Disability Rights Movement¶

    The movement advocating for the legal rights, accessibility, and social inclusion of people with disabilities.

    The disability rights movement led to landmark legislation such as the 1990 Americans with Disabilities Act, addressing ableism in public life.

    Example: A 1977 sit-in at a federal building in San Francisco pressured the government to enforce disability rights regulations.

    Discrimination¶

    The unfair treatment of a person or group based on characteristics such as race, gender, or religion rather than individual merit.

    Discrimination turns a negative attitude into action, whether through individual behavior or institutional policy.

    Example: Refusing to rent an apartment to a family because of their ethnicity is a documented historical pattern of discrimination.

    Displacement¶

    The condition of being forced to leave one's home or homeland, whether due to conflict, disaster, economic pressure, or government action.

    Displacement is a broader term than forced migration, since it can occur within a country's own borders, not only across them.

    Example: Families forced from their homes by a government dam or highway project, even without crossing a national border, experience displacement.

    Dominant Narrative¶

    The widely accepted version of a historical event or period, usually reflecting the perspective of the most powerful or best-documented group involved.

    This course repeatedly contrasts the dominant narrative with the accounts of marginalized groups left out of it.

    Example: The dominant narrative of westward expansion as settlement of empty land ignored the Indigenous nations already living there.

    Due Process¶

    The constitutional guarantee that government must follow fair legal procedures before depriving a person of life, liberty, or property.

    Historical denial of due process to marginalized groups, such as violence carried out without trial, is a documented violation of this constitutional principle.

    Example: A person arrested and held for weeks without formal charges or a hearing has been denied due process.

    Economic Exclusion¶

    The systematic denial of a group's access to jobs, credit, property, or markets, limiting its ability to build wealth.

    Economic exclusion has taken many specific forms in U.S. history, including discriminatory lending, hiring, and property practices.

    Economic Mobility¶

    The ability of a person or family to improve their financial and social standing across their lifetime or between generations.

    Economic mobility is often used to measure whether a society's opportunities are genuinely open or constrained by inherited disadvantage.

    Example: A child born into poverty who attains a stable middle-class career demonstrates upward economic mobility.

    Embargo¶

    A government order restricting or banning trade with a specific country or group, typically used as a political or economic pressure tool.

    Embargoes illustrate how economic exclusion can operate at the level of entire nations, not only individuals.

    Example: The U.S. trade embargo on Cuba, in place since the early 1960s, has restricted commerce between the two countries for decades.

    Emotional Appeal¶

    A persuasive technique that uses feelings such as fear, sympathy, or anger, rather than evidence, to influence an audience's opinion.

    Emotional appeal is a common propaganda tool, and recognizing it is a core media literacy skill.

    Example: Wartime posters depicting enemies as monstrous relied on emotional appeal to build public support for war.

    Empathy In History¶

    An informed emotional understanding of historical actors' experiences, built from evidence rather than from projecting modern feelings onto the past.

    Empathy in history differs from simple sympathy because it requires grounding feelings in documented context, not assumption.

    Equity Vs Equality¶

    The distinction between giving everyone identical resources, called equality, and giving people resources matched to their differing needs to reach a fair outcome, called equity.

    This distinction matters when evaluating policies meant to address historical disadvantage, since equal treatment alone may not undo unequal starting points.

    Example: Directing extra tutoring funding to underfunded schools, rather than equal funding to every school, is an equity-based approach.

    Erased Indigenous Voices¶

    The historical exclusion of Indigenous perspectives, testimony, and authorship from mainstream accounts of events that directly affected Indigenous nations.

    Restoring erased Indigenous voices requires seeking tribal records, oral tradition, and contemporary Indigenous scholarship often absent from older textbooks.

    Example: Early textbook accounts of the Trail of Tears rarely quoted Cherokee sources directly, an example of erased Indigenous voices.

    Erasure¶

    The removal or omission of a group's history, contributions, or existence from public memory, education, or media.

    Erasure often results from cumulative decisions about which stories get published, taught, or memorialized, rather than one deliberate act.

    Example: Omitting Chinese immigrants' role in building the transcontinental railroad from most textbooks is a case of erasure.

    Ethics Of Representation¶

    The set of principles guiding how a researcher should responsibly and respectfully portray the people, cultures, and events they study, especially marginalized groups.

    The ethics of representation asks researchers to consider whose voice tells a story and whether that telling could cause harm.

    Ethnicity¶

    A group identity based on shared ancestry, language, culture, or national origin, distinct from race, which is typically based on physical appearance.

    Ethnicity and race often overlap in how groups experience discrimination, but they describe different bases of identity.

    Example: Irish, Italian, and Polish Americans share a common racial classification in U.S. census categories but distinct ethnicities.

    Exhibit Design¶

    The planning and arrangement of physical or digital museum displays to present historical artifacts, images, and information to an audience.

    Exhibit design choices, such as which artifacts to feature and how to caption them, shape how visitors understand a historical topic.

    Federal Recognition¶

    The formal legal status the U.S. government grants to a tribal nation, establishing a government-to-government relationship and eligibility for certain protections and services.

    Federal recognition is a prerequisite for many treaty-based rights, and its denial has historically excluded some tribes from legal protections.

    Example: A tribe petitioning federal agencies for decades to document its continuous existence before gaining federal recognition illustrates the process's difficulty.

    Forced Migration¶

    The involuntary movement of people from their home region, caused by war, persecution, environmental disaster, or government policy.

    Forced migration differs from voluntary immigration because those displaced have little or no choice in leaving.

    Example: The transatlantic slave trade forcibly migrated millions of Africans to the Americas over several centuries.

    Framing Effect¶

    The way the specific wording or context used to present information influences how an audience interprets and judges it.

    The framing effect explains why identical facts can produce very different public reactions depending on how they are presented.

    Freedom Movement¶

    A collective effort by a marginalized group to secure basic rights and freedoms previously denied to them by law or custom.

    Freedom movement is often used interchangeably with civil rights movement but can also describe earlier abolitionist and anti-colonial efforts.

    Frontier Myth¶

    A cultural narrative portraying western expansion as an empty, untamed wilderness settled by heroic pioneers, which minimizes the presence and displacement of Indigenous peoples.

    The frontier myth shaped popular entertainment and education long after the historical events it describes.

    Example: Western films of the mid-twentieth century often depicted the frontier as vacant land, reinforcing the frontier myth.

    Fugitivity¶

    The historical condition and experience of escaping enslavement or unjust legal control, often used to describe enslaved people's flight toward freedom.

    Fugitivity reframes escape from slavery as an active act of resistance rather than simple absence from a plantation record.

    Example: Harriet Tubman's repeated journeys guiding enslaved people to freedom are studied as acts of fugitivity and resistance.

    Gender¶

    The socially constructed roles, behaviors, and expectations a society assigns to people, often but not always aligned with their sex assigned at birth.

    This course studies how gender expectations have shaped access to voting, employment, and legal rights throughout history.

    Example: Nineteenth-century laws barring women from most professions reflected gender roles enforced by law rather than by ability.

    Gentrification¶

    The process by which rising property values and new development in a neighborhood displace long-term, often lower-income residents.

    Gentrification frequently follows a period of community disinvestment, raising questions about who benefits from a neighborhood's later revival.

    Example: Rapid rent increases following new development in a historically Black urban neighborhood can trigger gentrification and displacement of longtime residents.

    Gerrymandering¶

    The deliberate drawing of electoral district boundaries to give one political party or group an unfair advantage in elections.

    Gerrymandering can be used to dilute the voting power of racial or ethnic minority communities.

    Example: Splitting a concentrated minority neighborhood across several districts to prevent it from electing a preferred candidate is a gerrymandering tactic.

    Global Migration Pattern¶

    A recurring, large-scale trend in human movement across borders, identified by studying causes such as economic opportunity, conflict, or environmental change.

    Studying global migration patterns helps situate specific historical episodes, such as forced migration, within broader worldwide trends.

    Example: The steady movement of workers from rural regions to industrializing cities across many countries is a recurring global migration pattern.

    Government Trust¶

    The degree of confidence citizens hold that government institutions will act fairly and in the public interest.

    Government trust among marginalized groups has often been shaped by documented experiences of discriminatory policy and enforcement.

    Grassroots Organizing¶

    Political or social organizing built from the efforts of ordinary community members, rather than directed from established leadership or institutions.

    Grassroots organizing has been essential to nearly every major social movement studied in this course, from suffrage to labor rights.

    Example: Local volunteers going door-to-door to register Black voters during the 1964 Freedom Summer campaign exemplify grassroots organizing.

    Headline Bias¶

    A slant introduced through the specific words and framing a news outlet chooses for a story's headline, shaping reader perception before the article is even read.

    Headline bias is a focused example of the broader framing effect, since headlines carry disproportionate influence on how a story is remembered.

    Example: Describing the same protest as a "riot" in one headline and a "demonstration" in another shows headline bias.

    Hidden History¶

    Historical events, groups, or perspectives that were deliberately or inadvertently left out of mainstream historical accounts, despite documentary evidence that they occurred.

    This course centers hidden history to show that historical knowledge is shaped by whose stories were recorded and preserved.

    Example: The wartime contributions of the Navajo Code Talkers remained hidden history until their work was declassified decades later.

    Hinduism¶

    A major religious and cultural tradition originating in the Indian subcontinent, encompassing diverse beliefs, deities, and practices developed over thousands of years.

    Hinduism is examined in this course for its historical role in shaping South Asian societies and its diaspora communities worldwide.

    Example: Temples built across South and Southeast Asia over centuries reflect Hinduism's long historical influence on regional art and architecture.

    Historical Context¶

    The surrounding political, social, economic, and cultural conditions of a time period that shape why events happened and how people understood them.

    Reading a source without its historical context risks misapplying present-day values to judge past actions and beliefs.

    Example: Understanding 1930s labor laws requires the historical context of the Great Depression's mass unemployment.

    Historical Documentary¶

    A nonfiction film or media production that uses evidence such as archival footage, interviews, and documents to present a historical topic.

    A historical documentary can serve as both a primary source about its own era of production and a secondary interpretation of its historical subject.

    Example: A documentary compiling oral history interviews with civil rights activists functions as both historical evidence and interpretation.

    Historical Silence¶

    A gap in the historical record where evidence about an event, group, or experience was never recorded, was destroyed, or was suppressed.

    Historians address historical silence by seeking indirect evidence, such as material culture or oral tradition, to fill the gap.

    Example: The near-total absence of enslaved people's own written testimony from the colonial era is a historical silence.

    Historiography¶

    The study of how history has been written, including the methods, sources, and assumptions historians use to select, interpret, and present the past.

    Understanding historiography helps students see that history is an evolving interpretation shaped by evidence and perspective, not a fixed set of facts.

    Homestead Act¶

    An 1862 federal law granting 160 acres of land to settlers who worked it for five years, drawn largely from land previously held by Indigenous nations.

    The Homestead Act accelerated westward expansion and the associated loss of Indigenous land.

    Housing Discrimination¶

    The unequal treatment of people seeking to buy, rent, or finance housing based on race, ethnicity, gender, or other protected characteristics.

    Housing discrimination includes redlining but also covers practices like refusing to rent or steering buyers away from certain neighborhoods.

    Example: Real estate agents historically steering Black homebuyers away from white neighborhoods is a documented form of housing discrimination.

    Identity Formation¶

    The ongoing process by which a person develops a sense of who they are, shaped by family, culture, community, and historical experience.

    This course considers how marginalization and forced cultural change can complicate identity formation for members of affected groups.

    Ideology In Media¶

    The underlying political or social belief system that shapes how a media outlet selects and presents information.

    Identifying ideology in media helps students understand why the same event can be reported very differently by different outlets.

    Example: A news outlet consistently framing immigration as either a security threat or a humanitarian issue reveals its underlying ideology.

    Immigration Policy¶

    The body of laws and regulations a government establishes to control who may enter, remain in, and become a citizen of a country.

    Immigration policy has historically included restrictions based on race and national origin, shaping which groups could enter the country.

    Example: The 1882 Chinese Exclusion Act barred nearly all immigration from China, an early instance of race-based immigration policy.

    Imperialism¶

    The extension of a nation's power and influence over other territories or peoples through military, political, or economic means, whether by direct colonization or indirect control.

    Imperialism is a broader concept than colonialism, since it can include economic or political domination without formal settlement.

    Example: The United States' acquisition of the Philippines after the 1898 Spanish-American War extended American political control without full colonization.

    Implicit Bias¶

    An unconscious attitude or stereotype that influences a person's judgments and actions without their conscious awareness.

    Implicit bias is studied as a possible contributor to disparities in policing, hiring, and sentencing, distinct from deliberate prejudice.

    Example: A hiring manager unconsciously rating identical resumes higher when the applicant's name sounds white rather than Black demonstrates implicit bias.

    Inclusion¶

    The practice of actively incorporating diverse groups into decision-making, institutions, and public narratives on equal terms.

    Inclusion goes beyond simple presence in a room, requiring that included groups have genuine influence over outcomes.

    Independent Statehood¶

    The legal and political status a territory achieves when it gains full sovereign control over its own governance, separate from a colonizing power.

    Independent statehood is often the outcome sought by anti-colonial movements following extended periods of colonial rule.

    Example: India's 1947 transition from British colonial rule to an independent nation marked its achievement of independent statehood.

    Indigenous Displacement¶

    The forced removal of Indigenous peoples from their traditional lands through war, treaty, or government policy.

    Indigenous displacement was carried out through specific federal laws and policies, not as an incidental byproduct of settlement.

    Example: The 1830 Indian Removal Act authorized the displacement of Southeastern nations to lands west of the Mississippi River.

    Indigenous Iconography¶

    Visual symbols, images, and imagery associated with Indigenous peoples, which are sometimes used respectfully and sometimes appropriated or stereotyped by outside groups.

    Indigenous iconography appears in this course's media unit as an example of representation that can be either authentic or reductive.

    Example: The use of Indigenous headdress imagery as sports team mascots has been widely criticized as a misuse of Indigenous iconography.

    Indigenous Resistance¶

    The range of actions Indigenous peoples have taken, from armed conflict to legal advocacy, to defend their land, culture, and sovereignty.

    Indigenous resistance spans centuries and includes both historical armed conflict and modern legal and political activism.

    Example: The 2016 Standing Rock protests against a pipeline crossing tribal land are a recent instance of Indigenous resistance.

    Indigenous Sovereignty¶

    The inherent right of Indigenous nations to govern their own people, land, and internal affairs, independent of external control.

    Indigenous sovereignty is the broader principle underlying tribal sovereignty and is recognized, though often contested, in U.S. and international law.

    Intergenerational Trauma¶

    Psychological and social harm from a historical injustice that continues to affect the descendants of those who directly experienced it.

    Intergenerational trauma is used to explain lasting effects of events such as slavery and the boarding school era on descendant communities.

    Intersectionality¶

    A framework showing that a person's overlapping identities, such as race and gender, combine to create distinct experiences of advantage or disadvantage not explained by either identity alone.

    Intersectionality helps explain why, for example, Black women's historical experience of discrimination differed from that of Black men or white women.

    Example: Domestic workers excluded from early minimum-wage laws were disproportionately Black women, an intersectional gap missed by looking at race or gender alone.

    Interview Technique¶

    The specific methods and skills a researcher uses to conduct an effective interview, including question design, active listening, and follow-up.

    Sound interview technique helps ensure that oral history research produces reliable, detailed, and respectful accounts.

    Islam¶

    A religious tradition founded in the seventh century based on the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad and the Quran, practiced by diverse communities worldwide.

    This course studies Islam's historical spread and the experience of Muslim communities facing religious persecution in various periods and regions.

    Example: Trade networks across the medieval Sahara helped spread Islam into West African kingdoms such as Mali and Songhai.

    Jim Crow Era¶

    The period from the late 1870s through the 1960s when Southern states enforced legal racial segregation through state and local laws.

    The Jim Crow Era followed the rollback of Reconstruction-era protections and required the Civil Rights Movement to dismantle it.

    Example: Laws requiring separate railroad cars for Black and white passengers were common statutes of the Jim Crow Era.

    Judaism¶

    The religious tradition and culture of the Jewish people, based on the Hebrew Bible and rabbinic teachings, with a documented history of both community continuity and persecution.

    Judaism's history includes repeated episodes of religious persecution, making it relevant to this course's broader study of marginalized groups.

    Example: Jewish communities maintained religious practice across centuries of dispersal following ancient expulsions from their historic homeland.

    Juvenile Justice¶

    The system of laws and institutions that specifically address offenses committed by minors, distinct from the adult criminal justice system.

    Juvenile justice policy is closely tied to the school-to-prison pipeline, since disciplinary referrals can funnel students into this system.

    Labor Exploitation¶

    The practice of extracting excessive work or value from workers while paying them unfairly low wages or denying basic protections.

    Labor exploitation has historically targeted immigrant, enslaved, and minority workers who had limited legal recourse to resist it.

    Example: Early twentieth-century factory owners paying immigrant workers far below a living wage for dangerous, long shifts practiced labor exploitation.

    Labor Rights Movement¶

    The organized effort by workers to secure fair wages, safe conditions, and the right to unionize, often through strikes and legislative advocacy.

    The labor rights movement intersected with immigrant and minority workers' experiences of labor exploitation discussed elsewhere in this course.

    Example: The 1911 Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire, which killed 146 workers, spurred labor rights movement demands for workplace safety regulation.

    Land Acknowledgment¶

    A formal statement recognizing the Indigenous nations who historically inhabited or continue to inhabit a particular piece of land.

    Land acknowledgment is a contemporary practice intended to recognize ongoing Indigenous presence and historical dispossession, though its impact is debated.

    Example: A public event opening with a statement naming the Dakota and Ojibwe nations as the land's original inhabitants is a land acknowledgment.

    Land Dispossession¶

    The loss of land ownership or use rights by a group, typically through legal, military, or economic force exerted by a more powerful party.

    Land dispossession is a broader term than Indigenous displacement, applying to any group's loss of land, including through discriminatory lending or eminent domain.

    Language And Power¶

    The relationship between a society's dominant language and the political, economic, and social advantages granted to its speakers over speakers of other languages.

    This unit examines how language policy has historically been used to include or exclude groups from full participation in society.

    Example: Laws requiring government business to be conducted solely in one language can disadvantage residents who are not native speakers of it.

    Language Revitalization¶

    Organized efforts to restore, strengthen, or increase the number of speakers of an endangered or suppressed language.

    Language revitalization projects are underway among many Indigenous nations seeking to counter the effects of past linguistic suppression.

    Example: Immersion schools teaching Ojibwe to children are part of a language revitalization effort among Minnesota Tribal Nations.

    Legacy Admissions¶

    A college admissions practice that gives preference to applicants whose relatives attended the same institution.

    Legacy admissions have been criticized for reinforcing existing advantages, since historically excluded groups had fewer alumni relatives to benefit from the policy.

    Legacy Of Slavery¶

    The ongoing social, economic, and political effects of the U.S. system of chattel slavery that persist after its 1865 legal abolition.

    The legacy of slavery is cited by historians as a contributing cause of later systemic racism, including in wealth and criminal justice disparities.

    Example: Disparities in Black and white household wealth today are traced by many historians partly to the legacy of slavery and its aftermath.

    Legislative Change¶

    The process by which laws are formally amended, repealed, or newly enacted, often as an outcome of sustained political pressure or advocacy.

    This course traces how social movements convert activism into concrete legislative change, such as the Voting Rights Act following civil rights organizing.

    LGBTQ Rights Movement¶

    The ongoing movement advocating for the legal and social equality of lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, and queer people.

    The LGBTQ rights movement gained national visibility after the 1969 Stonewall uprising and has achieved milestones including marriage equality.

    Example: The 1969 Stonewall uprising in New York City is widely regarded as a catalyzing event for the modern LGBTQ rights movement.

    Liberation Struggle¶

    A sustained effort by an oppressed group to end systemic domination and gain control over its own political, social, or economic future.

    Liberation struggle is a broad umbrella term this course applies across civil rights, anti-colonial, and Indigenous sovereignty movements.

    Linguistic Suppression¶

    The deliberate restriction or banning of a minority language by a government or institution, often to promote a dominant language and culture.

    Linguistic suppression was a central tool of the boarding school era, where Indigenous children were punished for speaking their native languages.

    Example: Wales once banned Welsh-language instruction in schools in favor of English, a documented case of linguistic suppression.

    Literacy Test¶

    A historical requirement that voters demonstrate reading or interpretive skill before registering, applied selectively to disenfranchise Black voters in the American South.

    Literacy tests were often administered unfairly, with white registrars applying impossible standards only to Black applicants.

    Example: Some Southern registrars asked Black applicants to interpret obscure passages of state constitutions while waiving the test for white applicants.

    Manifest Destiny¶

    A nineteenth-century belief that the United States was divinely or morally entitled to expand its territory across North America.

    Manifest Destiny provided ideological justification for displacing Indigenous nations during westward expansion.

    Example: Political cartoons of the 1840s depicted westward expansion as an inevitable, righteous march across the continent.

    Marginalization¶

    The process by which a group is pushed to the edges of social, political, or economic power and denied full participation in institutions.

    Marginalization can occur through law, custom, or economic structures, and it often compounds across multiple areas of life at once.

    Example: Denying women the right to vote until 1920 was one form of political marginalization in U.S. history.

    Mass Incarceration¶

    The historically unprecedented rise in the U.S. imprisoned population since the 1970s, which disproportionately affects Black and Latino communities.

    Mass incarceration is linked by researchers to policy choices such as mandatory minimum sentences and aggressive drug enforcement.

    Example: The U.S. prison population grew roughly fivefold between 1970 and 2000, a rise researchers link to mass incarceration policy.

    Media Literacy¶

    The ability to critically analyze, evaluate, and interpret media messages, including recognizing bias, framing, and persuasive technique.

    Media literacy is a practical skill this course builds throughout its unit on media representation and misinformation.

    Media Representation¶

    The way people, groups, or events are portrayed across news, film, television, and other media, and how accurately that portrayal reflects reality.

    Media representation shapes public perception of marginalized groups, sometimes reinforcing stereotypes and sometimes challenging them.

    Minnesota Tribal Nations¶

    The eleven federally recognized sovereign Indigenous nations located within the state of Minnesota, including Ojibwe and Dakota communities.

    Minnesota Tribal Nations offer a regional case study for examining treaty rights, sovereignty, and self-determination in a specific local context.

    Multiple Identities¶

    The condition of holding more than one social identity at once, such as race, gender, religion, and nationality, each shaping a person's experience.

    Multiple identities underlie intersectionality, since no person's experience is defined by a single category alone.

    Naming And Identity¶

    The relationship between a person's or group's name and their sense of self, heritage, and belonging, including the effects of renaming imposed by outside authorities.

    Naming and identity connects to forced cultural change, since renaming practices have historically been used to erase a group's original culture.

    Example: Boarding school staff assigning English names to Indigenous children in place of their given names is a documented example involving naming and identity.

    Narrative Reconstruction¶

    The scholarly process of piecing together a coherent historical account from fragmentary, incomplete, or previously overlooked evidence.

    Narrative reconstruction is central to recovering absent narratives, since it requires assembling evidence that was never organized into a single account.

    Nation-To-Nation Relations¶

    The diplomatic and legal relationship between the U.S. federal government and Indigenous tribal nations, based on treaties rather than domestic law alone.

    Nation-to-nation relations recognize tribes as sovereign governments, distinct from the relationship the federal government has with states.

    Nationalism¶

    A strong sense of identity and loyalty to one's nation, often including the belief that national interests should take priority over other loyalties.

    Nationalism can foster unity but has also historically been used to justify excluding groups considered outside the national identity.

    Example: Nineteenth-century movements to unify Germany and Italy around a shared national identity were driven by nationalism.

    Nationality¶

    A person's legal membership in a nation-state, typically established through birth, descent, or naturalization, granting specific rights and obligations.

    Nationality shapes legal status and access to rights, which is central to understanding migration and border history.

    Nonviolent Resistance¶

    A strategy of protest and social change that deliberately avoids violence, using methods such as boycotts, sit-ins, and marches to pressure those in power.

    Nonviolent resistance was a defining strategic choice of the Civil Rights Movement, associated closely with leaders like Martin Luther King Jr.

    Example: The 1960 Greensboro sit-ins, where students peacefully occupied segregated lunch counters, exemplify nonviolent resistance.

    Oral History Method¶

    A research approach that gathers historical information through recorded interviews with people who directly experienced or witnessed past events.

    The oral history method is especially valuable for documenting the experiences of groups whose stories were rarely recorded in official written archives.

    Example: Recording interviews with elders who lived through the boarding school era preserves firsthand accounts using the oral history method.

    Oral Tradition¶

    The practice of preserving and transmitting a community's history, values, and stories through spoken word across generations rather than through writing.

    Oral tradition is an essential source for reconstructing the history of groups whose written records were destroyed, suppressed, or never created.

    Example: Many Indigenous nations preserved detailed historical accounts through oral tradition long before written record-keeping was introduced to their communities.

    Peer Review¶

    The process of having other researchers or classmates evaluate a piece of work for accuracy, clarity, and quality before it is finalized.

    Peer review helps identify gaps in evidence or reasoning that the original researcher may have missed.

    Perspective-Taking¶

    The deliberate effort to understand a historical event or issue from the standpoint of someone whose experience differs from one's own.

    Perspective-taking supports informed empathy by asking students to reconstruct how events looked to the people who lived them.

    Example: Reading a Japanese American internee's letters is one way to practice perspective-taking about World War II policy.

    Policing History¶

    The study of how law enforcement institutions developed over time, including their origins and their historical relationship to marginalized communities.

    Policing history in parts of the United States includes roots in slave patrols, which shapes ongoing debates about policing today.

    Policy Proposal¶

    A structured written recommendation for a specific government or institutional action, supported by evidence and reasoning, intended to address an identified problem.

    A policy proposal applies historical research to a present-day issue by recommending a concrete course of action.

    Example: A written recommendation calling for restored voting rights for formerly incarcerated citizens is a policy proposal.

    Political Boundary¶

    A line, whether natural or artificial, that marks the legal limit of a jurisdiction such as a nation, state, or reservation.

    Political boundaries drawn without Indigenous input have repeatedly divided or diminished tribal territory.

    Political Persuasion¶

    The use of argument, rhetoric, or appeals to influence public opinion or voting behavior on a political issue.

    Political persuasion can rely on evidence and reasoning or, when unsupported by fact, shade into misleading propaganda.

    Poll Tax¶

    A fee historically required to vote, used in the American South to disenfranchise poor voters, disproportionately affecting Black citizens.

    The 24th Amendment abolished poll taxes in federal elections in 1964, decades after Southern states began using them for suppression.

    Example: Mississippi's poll tax required voters to pay a fee at registration, a cost many poor Black and white sharecroppers could not afford.

    Power Structure¶

    The arrangement of institutions, laws, and social roles through which a society distributes authority and decision-making control.

    Power structures determine which groups can shape laws, media, and economic systems, and which groups must respond to decisions made by others.

    Prejudice¶

    A preconceived negative attitude or judgment toward a person, formed because of their membership in a particular group rather than their individual actions.

    Prejudice describes an attitude, which is distinct from discrimination, the act of treating someone unfairly because of that attitude.

    Prejudice Statistics¶

    Quantitative data used to measure and document patterns of bias or discrimination across a population, such as arrest rates or lending approvals.

    Prejudice statistics allow researchers to identify systemic patterns that individual anecdotes alone cannot establish.

    Example: Comparing traffic-stop rates by driver race across a city is a way to compile prejudice statistics.

    Presenting Findings¶

    The process of communicating research results clearly to an audience through writing, visuals, or oral presentation.

    Presenting findings effectively is the final step connecting a capstone project's research to a genuine public audience.

    Primary Source¶

    A firsthand document, artifact, or record created during the time period being studied, such as a letter, photograph, or government record.

    Primary sources let historians examine evidence directly rather than relying on someone else's interpretation of events.

    Example: A soldier's diary written during the Civil War is a primary source for studying that conflict.

    Privilege¶

    An unearned advantage granted to members of a particular group by social structures, often unnoticed by those who hold it.

    Studying privilege alongside marginalization helps explain why the same policy can affect different groups very differently.

    Example: Being able to easily find bandages marketed to match one's own skin tone is a small but real form of privilege.

    Propaganda¶

    Information, often biased or misleading, deliberately spread to promote a particular political cause, viewpoint, or leader.

    Propaganda differs from ordinary persuasion because it typically relies on emotional appeal and selective or false information rather than balanced argument.

    Example: World War II government posters urging citizens to conserve resources for the war effort combined patriotic emotional appeal with propaganda techniques.

    Public History¶

    The practice of presenting historical research to a general, non-academic audience through museums, documentaries, monuments, and community programs.

    Public history projects, including exhibit design and historical documentaries, are common ways this course's research skills reach a wider audience.

    Example: A local historical society's walking tour of a formerly redlined neighborhood is a form of public history.

    Race¶

    A social category historically assigned to groups of people based on perceived physical characteristics, used to justify differential treatment despite lacking a firm biological basis.

    Scientists broadly agree that race is a social construction rather than a biological classification, yet it has had powerful real-world consequences.

    Example: U.S. law once classified people by race to determine who could vote, own property, or marry freely.

    Racial Capitalism¶

    An economic system in which racial hierarchy is used to extract greater profit or value from the labor of a subordinated racial group.

    Racial capitalism links economic exclusion directly to the maintenance of racial hierarchy, rather than treating the two as separate issues.

    Example: Enslaved labor generating profit for plantation owners without wages is a foundational example of racial capitalism in U.S. history.

    Racial Hierarchy¶

    A ranked ordering of racial groups within a society that assigns unequal status, rights, or privileges based on perceived race.

    Racial hierarchies were often written directly into law, such as citizenship and immigration statutes, shaping opportunity for generations.

    Example: Nineteenth-century U.S. naturalization law limited citizenship to "free white persons," codifying a racial hierarchy in federal law.

    Racial Profiling¶

    The practice of law enforcement targeting individuals for suspicion or stops primarily based on their race rather than specific evidence of wrongdoing.

    Racial profiling has been documented in traffic stops and searches, and it is a frequently cited example of selective enforcement.

    Example: Studies of urban traffic stops have found Black drivers stopped and searched at higher rates than white drivers under similar circumstances.

    Reconstruction Era¶

    The period from 1865 to 1877 following the Civil War, during which the U.S. government attempted to rebuild the South and extend rights to formerly enslaved people.

    The Reconstruction Era saw major legal gains for Black Americans that were later reversed by subsequent segregation laws.

    Example: The 14th and 15th Amendments, guaranteeing citizenship and voting rights regardless of race, were passed during the Reconstruction Era.

    Redistricting¶

    The periodic redrawing of electoral district boundaries, typically after a census, to reflect population changes.

    Redistricting is a neutral process in principle, but it becomes gerrymandering when boundaries are drawn to produce an unfair advantage.

    Redlining¶

    A discriminatory practice, formalized by federal mortgage maps in the 1930s, that denied loans and insurance to residents of neighborhoods marked as high-risk, mostly Black and immigrant areas.

    Redlining produced lasting effects on generational wealth because homeownership has historically been a primary way American families build assets.

    Example: The federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation's 1930s maps marked many Black neighborhoods in red as "hazardous," blocking residents from federally backed mortgages.

    Refugee Experience¶

    The set of circumstances, challenges, and losses faced by people who have fled their home country due to war, persecution, or disaster.

    The refugee experience often includes not only physical displacement but also legal uncertainty and loss of community.

    Example: Vietnamese families who fled by boat after 1975 and spent years in refugee camps before resettlement describe a difficult refugee experience.

    Religious Persecution¶

    The mistreatment or discrimination against people specifically because of their religious beliefs or practices, ranging from social exclusion to violence.

    Religious persecution has driven historical waves of forced migration and diaspora formation, and it remains a documented feature of many periods studied in this course.

    Example: Jewish communities faced organized violence and expulsion in various European countries across multiple centuries.

    Religious Pluralism¶

    The coexistence of multiple religious traditions within a society, along with the recognition and acceptance of that diversity by law or custom.

    Religious pluralism is contrasted in this course with historical periods of enforced religious conformity or persecution.

    Reparations¶

    Compensation, financial or otherwise, proposed or provided to a group for historical injustices committed against them or their ancestors.

    Reparations proposals are debated in relation to slavery, Indigenous land loss, and other documented historical harms.

    Example: Payments made to Japanese Americans interned during World War II under the 1988 Civil Liberties Act are a historical instance of reparations.

    Repatriation¶

    The return of cultural items, human remains, or artifacts to the Indigenous nation or community from which they were originally taken.

    Repatriation is a legal process in the U.S. under federal law requiring museums to return certain items to tribes that request them.

    Example: A museum returning ancestral remains to a tribal nation under federal repatriation law is a documented case of this practice.

    Representation¶

    The way a group is depicted or included in media, government, or historical accounts, and how accurately that depiction reflects the group's diversity.

    This course examines representation across government, media, and historiography as a recurring measure of a group's social standing.

    Research Question¶

    A clear, focused question that defines the specific issue a historical investigation seeks to answer, guiding what evidence to gather and analyze.

    A strong research question is specific enough to be answered with available sources, yet open enough to allow genuine investigation.

    Restorative Justice¶

    An approach to addressing wrongdoing that focuses on repairing harm to victims and communities through dialogue, rather than solely on punishment.

    Restorative justice is often proposed as an alternative to incarceration for certain offenses.

    Example: A program bringing a young offender and the person they harmed together to agree on repair, rather than jail time, applies restorative justice.

    School-To-Prison Pipeline¶

    A pattern in which harsh school discipline policies push students, disproportionately Black and disabled students, out of school and toward the juvenile justice system.

    The school-to-prison pipeline links education policy directly to later contact with the criminal justice system.

    Example: A student suspended repeatedly for minor infractions and later referred to law enforcement illustrates the school-to-prison pipeline in action.

    Secondary Source¶

    A work that analyzes, interprets, or summarizes primary sources, typically written after the events it describes by someone who did not directly experience them.

    Comparing primary and secondary sources reveals how interpretation can shift the meaning historians assign to the same evidence.

    Example: A textbook chapter summarizing the causes of the Trail of Tears is a secondary source.

    Selective Enforcement¶

    The practice of applying laws more strictly to some groups than others, even when the law itself is written neutrally.

    Selective enforcement shows that discrimination can occur in how a law is applied, independent of the law's written text.

    Example: Enforcing loitering laws more aggressively in minority neighborhoods than in similar white neighborhoods is a form of selective enforcement.

    Self-Determination¶

    The right of a people or nation to freely decide its own political status and pursue its own economic, social, and cultural development.

    Self-determination became official U.S. Indigenous policy in the 1970s, replacing earlier assimilationist approaches like the boarding school era.

    Example: The 1975 Indian Self-Determination Act allowed tribes to directly administer federal programs on their reservations rather than having federal agencies manage them.

    Self-Inquiry¶

    The practice of examining one's own assumptions, biases, and cultural position before interpreting historical evidence about other groups.

    Self-inquiry is a starting point for critical thinking, since unexamined assumptions can distort how a reader judges a source.

    Sense Of Place¶

    The emotional and cultural connection a person or community feels toward a particular physical location, often tied to history, memory, and belonging.

    Forced migration and land dispossession can sever a community's sense of place, disrupting cultural continuity across generations.

    Example: Indigenous nations removed from ancestral lands describe a lasting loss of sense of place even generations later.

    Sentencing Disparity¶

    A measurable difference in the length or severity of criminal sentences given to different groups for comparable offenses.

    Sentencing disparity has been documented between racial groups convicted of similar crimes, raising questions about fairness in the justice system.

    Example: Historical federal sentencing guidelines punished crack cocaine offenses far more harshly than powder cocaine offenses, producing a documented racial sentencing disparity.

    Settler Colonialism¶

    A form of colonialism in which settlers permanently occupy and replace an Indigenous population's land, society, and governance rather than only extracting resources.

    Settler colonialism differs from other colonial models because the settling population intends to remain and establish a new permanent society.

    Example: The establishment of permanent U.S. towns and farms on former Indigenous territory illustrates settler colonialism rather than temporary resource extraction.

    Sexuality¶

    A person's pattern of romantic or sexual attraction and their identity in relation to it, historically regulated by law, religion, and social custom.

    This course examines how legal and social treatment of sexuality has changed over time, particularly through modern rights movements.

    Shamanism¶

    A spiritual practice, found in many Indigenous cultures worldwide, in which a designated practitioner is believed to mediate between the human and spirit worlds.

    This course presents shamanism as a documented historical and cultural practice, without asserting the truth of its spiritual claims.

    Example: Siberian and some North American Indigenous communities have historically relied on a shaman to conduct healing or spiritual ceremonies.

    Social Construction¶

    An idea, category, or institution that exists because a society has collectively agreed to treat it as real, rather than because it reflects a fixed natural fact.

    Recognizing race, gender roles, and nationality as social constructions does not make their historical effects less real or less consequential.

    Social Construction Of Crime¶

    The idea that what counts as criminal behavior is defined by lawmakers and shaped by social attitudes, rather than reflecting an inherent moral wrong.

    This concept explains why the same act, such as drug possession, can carry very different penalties across time periods or jurisdictions.

    Example: Marijuana possession was a serious federal crime for decades before many states legalized it, showing the social construction of crime.

    Social Movement Strategy¶

    The deliberate set of tactics, such as protest, litigation, or lobbying, that a movement selects to achieve its goals.

    Comparing social movement strategy across different movements reveals why some campaigns favor legal challenges while others favor direct action.

    Source Bias¶

    A slant in a document or account that reflects the particular interests, assumptions, or limitations of the person or institution who created it.

    Recognizing source bias does not mean discarding a source; it means reading it critically alongside other evidence.

    Example: A newspaper owned by plantation interests describing an enslaved uprising likely shows source bias against the rebels.

    Source Corroboration¶

    The process of confirming a claim by comparing it against multiple independent sources to check for consistency and reliability.

    Source corroboration is especially important when studying marginalized groups, since a single account may be incomplete or contested.

    Source Triangulation¶

    A research practice of comparing three or more independent sources on the same topic to verify accuracy and identify points of agreement or conflict.

    Source triangulation builds on simpler two-source comparison by requiring multiple, varied types of evidence rather than just a single match.

    Source Validity¶

    The degree to which a source can be trusted as accurate, reliable, and relevant evidence for the claim it supports.

    Assessing source validity is essential when evaluating both historical documents and contemporary digital media claims.

    Sovereignty Erosion¶

    The gradual weakening of a tribal nation's self-governing authority through federal policy, court rulings, or resource extraction on its land.

    Sovereignty erosion shows that Indigenous sovereignty, though legally recognized, has been continually challenged rather than fully secure.

    Example: Federal approval of a pipeline crossing tribal land without full tribal consent illustrates sovereignty erosion.

    Stereotype¶

    An oversimplified and widely shared belief about the characteristics of a group that ignores individual differences among its members.

    Stereotypes can shape media portrayals, policy, and everyday treatment of a group, often without factual basis.

    Example: Portraying all Indigenous peoples as living only in the past ignores the diversity of contemporary Indigenous nations.

    Stereotype In Media¶

    The repeated portrayal of a group through a narrow, oversimplified set of traits across film, television, or advertising.

    Stereotypes in media can shape public assumptions about a group even among audiences with little direct contact with that group.

    Example: Repeatedly casting members of a particular ethnic group only as criminals in films reinforces a stereotype in media.

    Suffrage Movement¶

    The organized campaign to secure voting rights for groups excluded from the franchise, most notably the movement that won women the vote in 1920.

    The suffrage movement illustrates how sustained civic organizing can produce legislative and constitutional change over decades.

    Example: The 1848 Seneca Falls Convention launched decades of organizing that led to the 1920 constitutional amendment granting women the vote.

    Supranationalism¶

    A system in which independent nations transfer some decision-making authority to a shared international body that can bind its members.

    Supranationalism is relevant to understanding modern international bodies that shape migration and human rights policy across borders.

    Example: The European Union's authority to set trade rules binding on its member nations illustrates supranationalism.

    Systemic Oppression¶

    Disadvantage built into a society's laws, institutions, and everyday practices that consistently limits the rights or opportunities of a particular group.

    Unlike individual prejudice, systemic oppression operates through institutions and persists even when no single person intends harm.

    Example: Segregated public schools funded unequally by district functioned as systemic oppression regardless of any one official's intent.

    Systemic Racism¶

    Racial inequality embedded in laws, institutions, and everyday practices that produces unequal outcomes for racial groups, regardless of individual intent.

    Systemic racism can persist even after discriminatory laws are repealed, through practices such as unequal school funding formulas.

    Example: Mortgage-lending maps that marked minority neighborhoods as high-risk shaped lending decisions for decades after the maps were officially discontinued.

    Systemic Sexism¶

    Gender inequality embedded in laws, institutions, and everyday practices that limits opportunities for a gender, typically women, regardless of individual intent.

    Systemic sexism parallels systemic racism but operates along gender rather than racial lines, often through employment or property law.

    Example: Laws once requiring a husband's signature for a wife to open a bank account reflected systemic sexism.

    Tokenism¶

    The practice of including a small, symbolic number of people from a marginalized group to create an appearance of inclusion without real structural change.

    Tokenism differs from genuine inclusion because it leaves underlying power structures and decision-making unchanged.

    Example: A company that features one minority employee in its advertising while excluding minorities from leadership positions is practicing tokenism.

    Tokenized Casting¶

    The practice of including a small number of characters from marginalized groups in a production primarily to signal diversity, without developing them fully.

    Tokenized casting is the media-specific application of tokenism, often criticized for shallow or stereotyped character development.

    Example: A film featuring a single supporting character from a marginalized group who has no name or backstory illustrates tokenized casting.

    Trade Restriction¶

    A government-imposed limit on the import or export of goods, such as tariffs, quotas, or licensing requirements.

    Trade restrictions can be used to protect domestic industries or, historically, to enforce discriminatory economic policy against a targeted nation or group.

    Example: Tariffs placed on imported steel to protect domestic manufacturers are a common form of trade restriction.

    Trail Of Tears¶

    The forced 1830s relocation of the Cherokee and other Southeastern Indigenous nations to present-day Oklahoma, during which thousands died from exposure, disease, and starvation.

    The Trail of Tears is a documented case study of Indigenous displacement carried out under direct federal authority.

    Translation Bias¶

    A distortion in meaning that occurs when a text or speech is translated from one language to another, whether by error or by deliberate choice.

    Translation bias can shift the meaning of treaties and historical documents, complicating the historical record for groups whose testimony survives only in translation.

    Example: Early European translations of Indigenous treaty negotiations sometimes altered key terms, a documented case of translation bias affecting legal history.

    Treaty Rights¶

    The specific legal entitlements, such as hunting, fishing, or land rights, that Indigenous nations retain through formal agreements signed with the U.S. government.

    Treaty rights remain legally binding even when they are ignored or violated in practice, and many have been reaffirmed through litigation.

    Example: Ojibwe treaty rights to fish and hunt on ceded territory in the Great Lakes region have been upheld in federal court.

    Tribal Sovereignty¶

    The inherent legal and political authority of Indigenous nations to govern themselves, independent of state governments and predating the founding of the United States.

    Tribal sovereignty is recognized in the U.S. Constitution and numerous treaties, though it has been repeatedly limited by federal policy.

    U.S. Foreign Policy¶

    The strategies and actions the United States government adopts to guide its relationships and interactions with other nations.

    U.S. foreign policy has shaped migration patterns, border formation, and refugee flows throughout the country's history.

    Voter Intimidation¶

    The use of threats, harassment, or force to discourage eligible voters from casting a ballot.

    Voter intimidation was historically used alongside legal barriers to suppress turnout among Black voters in the American South.

    Example: Armed groups stationed at polling places to threaten Black voters in the Jim Crow South constituted voter intimidation.

    Voter Suppression¶

    Any strategy, legal or illegal, that makes it harder for eligible members of a group to register or vote.

    Voter suppression includes both direct intimidation and indirect barriers such as fees or unfair qualification tests.

    Voting Rights Act¶

    A 1965 federal law that banned discriminatory voting practices such as literacy tests and authorized federal oversight of election changes in areas with histories of discrimination.

    The Voting Rights Act directly followed sustained civil rights activism and remains a benchmark for evaluating later voting policy.

    Example: Federal examiners sent to register voters in Alabama counties in 1965 sharply increased Black voter registration within months.

    Wealth Gap¶

    The measurable difference in accumulated assets, such as savings, property, and investments, between different demographic groups.

    The wealth gap between racial groups in the United States is frequently traced by historians to policies like redlining and unequal access to credit.

    Example: Median Black household wealth in the United States has measured a small fraction of median white household wealth in recent national surveys.

    Westward Expansion¶

    The nineteenth-century growth of United States territory and settlement across North America, from the Atlantic coast toward the Pacific.

    Westward expansion is often taught as national progress, but this course also examines its costs to Indigenous nations already living on the land.

    Whitewashing¶

    The practice of casting white actors in roles originally written for or historically based on people of color, or removing racial context from a story.

    Whitewashing has been documented across decades of film history and is frequently cited in discussions of media representation.

    Example: Casting a white actor to portray a historically Asian or Indigenous figure in a film is a documented instance of whitewashing.
